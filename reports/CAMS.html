What CAMS Does, and Where It Stands Among Its Rivals

A Registrar and Transfer Agent, or RTA, is the back-office record-keeper for a mutual fund. Every time an investor buys, sells, or switches a fund, that transaction has to be recorded, every folio has to be tracked, and every investor's identity has to be verified under India's KYC (Know Your Customer) rules. Mutual fund companies (AMCs, or Asset Management Companies) outsource this work to an RTA rather than build it themselves. The AMC pays a fee, typically tied to the size of the assets serviced, for this service.

This is a licensed business. SEBI, India's securities regulator, decides who may operate as an RTA. As of today, only two firms handle nearly all of this work nationally: CAMS and KFin Technologies. This license, and the deep, hard-to-replicate infrastructure built up over decades of processing investor records, is the main barrier that keeps this a two-player market.

CAMS was incorporated in Chennai on 25 May 1988, founded by V. Shankar, originally to digitize investor records as India prepared to open up its capital markets. It received its first SEBI registration as a Registrar in 1993, and an upgraded, full registration in 1995. HDFC Group invested in the company in 2000. The National Stock Exchange (NSE) took a stake in 2014, then was ordered by SEBI in 2020 to divest it, on the grounds that a stock exchange should not also own a firm exposed to the mutual fund industry it regulates access to. CAMS listed on the stock exchanges in October 2020.

Private equity firm Warburg Pincus, through an entity called Great Terrain Investment, first bought into CAMS in 2017 (acquiring HDFC's stake) and built its holding up to just under 20% by 2023. It fully exited on 4 December 2023, selling its entire 19.87% stake for ₹2,700 crore in an open-market transaction to a group of global and domestic institutional investors. As of the most recent disclosure (June 2026), CAMS has no promoter at all — the shareholding is 44.88% foreign institutional investors, 23.54% domestic institutional investors, and 31.56% public. This is the same ownership structure this project's CARE Ratings report found for that company: professionally run, with no controlling owner, after its original financial-institution backers sold down over time.

The current Managing Director and CEO, Anuj Kumar, was re-appointed for a fresh five-year term effective August 2026 — a signal of continuity in strategy and leadership.

CAMS is the larger, more dominant, and financially stronger of the two — nearly double KFin's return on capital, and roughly twice its market share — yet it trades at a meaningfully lower earnings multiple than the smaller player. This is a genuinely favorable relative-value position, not something every "cheaper stock vs. its rival" comparison can claim.

KFin Technologies has its own notable history. It began as Karvy Computershare, the RTA arm of the Hyderabad-based Karvy Group. In 2018, private equity firm General Atlantic bought an 83.5% stake. In 2019, a major scandal broke at a separate entity in the same group, Karvy Stock Broking, over alleged misuse of client securities. To distance itself from that controversy, General Atlantic renamed the RTA business KFin Technologies in December 2019. KFin listed independently on the stock exchanges in December 2022.

Structural tailwinds reinforcing this duopoly: SEBI has discussed raising the minimum net worth required to register as a "qualified" RTA, to as much as ₹10 crore — a change that raises the bar for any new entrant, not the two incumbents. Every new AMC that gets a fresh SEBI license is also a potential new RTA client in a market CAMS already dominates: CAMS won six new AMC mandates in FY26 (including Angel One, Torus Oro Wealth, and Unifi Capital) and named five more going live before December 2026 (AlphaGrep, ASK, Carnelian, Oaklane, and Neo).

Debt/Equity has never exceeded 0.16 in ten years and has fallen every year since FY2021. "Borrowings" appear on the balance sheet for the first time in FY2021, on an otherwise debt-free business, with modest associated interest expense (₹6–8cr a year). This pattern is consistent with Ind AS 116, a lease-accounting rule introduced around FY2020 that requires companies to record long-term office leases as a balance-sheet liability for the first time — not confirmed directly from CAMS's own disclosures, but the timing matches, and this project's CARE Ratings report found the identical pattern for the identical reason.

Receivable days — the average time CAMS takes to collect fees owed to it — sat in a tight, low 7–17 day band for nine of the last ten years. FY2025 broke that pattern: receivables roughly doubled (₹49.1cr → ₹100.8cr), pushing collection time to 27.6 days, more than 60% above anything seen in the prior nine years. FY2026 then reversed it just as sharply, back down to 9.6 days — the lowest of the whole decade. Neither the annual reports nor the four earnings calls reviewed for this report explain what caused this one-year round trip. It could be a fiscal-year-end billing-cycle timing effect. It could be something else. It remains open.

Capital work in progress (CWIP) — money spent on buildings, systems, or infrastructure not yet finished and put to use — was negligible through FY2024 (under 6% of the fixed-asset base) and then rose sharply: 18.6% of the fixed-asset base in FY2025, 30.3% in FY2026. This is a real, escalating capital-investment phase. No annual report or transcript reviewed for this report discloses a specific completion date for whatever this spending is building. The most likely connection: CAMS has separately disclosed a roughly ₹500cr, multi-year technology re-architecture project (about ₹123cr spent as of early 2026), and management said on an earnings call that cloud and data-center costs are "becoming expensive faster than we thought." This is a plausible link, not a confirmed one — no source ties the balance-sheet CWIP figure to that specific project by name.

Where the Money Went in FY2026, and One Real Gap

A sources-and-uses check tracks every rupee of cash in against every rupee out, and confirms the totals tie to the company's own reported net change in cash.

For FY2026: cash from operations (₹577.7cr) and a small equity inflow from employee stock options (₹0.2cr) were the sources. Uses were an estimated ₹141.2cr of capital spending (the CWIP build discussed above), ₹18.7cr of debt repayment, and an estimated ₹291.1cr of dividends and other shareholder payouts. That leaves a genuine gap: roughly ₹129.5cr of investing cash flow that this report's data sources cannot explain — it doesn't match capital spending, and it doesn't match the change in the disclosed "Investments" balance-sheet line either. The totals still tie out exactly to CAMS's reported net cash change of −₹2.6cr; what's missing is a clean breakdown of one piece inside that total. This is flagged here as an open data gap, not glossed over — it deserves a specific explanation in CAMS's own cash-flow notes that this report's data sources do not provide.

One more balance-sheet line stands out over the decade: "Other Assets" grew from ₹85.2cr (FY2017) to ₹682.5cr (FY2026), roughly an eightfold increase, while the separately disclosed "Investments" line also grew, from ₹305.5cr to ₹570.8cr. Both growing together, rather than one growing at the other's expense, means this cannot be explained as a simple reclassification between the two lines. Plausible candidates include unbilled revenue on a fast-growing services business, a right-of-use asset tied to the same lease accounting that introduced "Borrowings" in FY2021, or deferred tax assets. None of these is confirmed by any source available to this report. It would take CAMS's own detailed notes to accounts to resolve.

Related-Party Dealings: Checked and Clean

This is an area many reports in this project have had to leave unanswered, because a standard data export simply doesn't carry it. For CAMS, the four annual reports read directly for this analysis (FY2021-22, FY2022-23, FY2023-24, FY2025-26) each contain a specific related-party disclosure note, and all four say the same thing: CAMS's related-party exposure is entirely with its own wholly-owned subsidiaries (an insurance-repository unit, a KYC-registration unit, a payments unit, and others), consisting of small equity investments, minor trade payables, and — new in FY2026 — two small loans to two subsidiaries (₹4.0cr to Think Analytics India, ₹1.9cr to Fintuple Technologies). Every year states these balances are unsecured, at arm's length, with nothing written off. No promoter, director, or non-subsidiary related-party balance appears in any of the four years. This is a genuine, verified pass across four years of primary-source filings — one new lending channel opened in FY2026 with no multi-year track record yet, worth watching, but nothing resembling fund diversion.

The Piotroski Health-Trend Test

The Piotroski F-Score is a published (Piotroski, 2000) test that checks nine specific year-over-year financial signals, not a company's absolute quality level — a stable, already-excellent business can score poorly here simply because it has little room left to keep "improving" by these strict tests. This project's CARE Ratings report found exactly that pattern; the same caution applies here.

CAMS scores 5 of 8 applicable tests (one of the nine published tests needs a current-assets/current-liabilities split this data source doesn't carry, for any company in this project). It passes: positive net income, positive operating cash flow, operating cash flow exceeding net income, falling leverage, and a gross margin that improved — though that last one barely moved, from 81.96% to 81.98%, essentially flat rather than a real gain. It fails three tests: return on assets fell (28.3% in FY2026 versus 30.3% in FY2025 — consistent with the profit-growth stall already discussed), share count rose very slightly (0.34%, almost certainly routine employee stock option exercises, not a real capital raise), and asset turnover fell marginally (0.914x versus 0.916x). None of the three fails points to a governance or capital-discipline problem — they mostly restate the same FY2026 profitability pause already visible in the raw numbers above.

How Healthy Is the Business

Revenue quality. CAMS earns most of its revenue from mutual fund back-office fees, tied to the assets it services on behalf of AMC clients. A smaller but consistently faster-growing slice comes from non-mutual-fund lines: payments processing, an insurance-record-keeping repository, KYC registration services, alternative-investment-fund servicing, pension record-keeping, and account aggregation. All of these grew 20%+ year over year across every one of the four quarters reviewed. The core business moves with two things: how much money Indian households are putting into mutual funds, and how many new fund houses and products CAMS can sign up as clients.

Profitability: a strong decade with one real, unresolved wobble. Operating margin ranged from a low of 25.4% (FY2019) to a high of 41.4% (FY2025), before falling to 38.8% in FY2026 on the unexplained expense jump discussed above. There is a genuine historical precedent worth knowing here. SEBI cut mutual fund expense ratios once before, in September 2018 — a change that hit AMC industry profits by an estimated 12–25% at the time. CAMS's own net margin fell sharply that same fiscal year (FY2019: 17.2%, down from 26.4% the year before) and recovered within about a year (FY2020: 24.8%; FY2021: 32.5%). This is real evidence that CAMS has weathered a comparable regulatory shock before and come out the other side — though it is evidence, not a guarantee, for how the current regulatory change plays out.

Working capital: clean for most of the decade, with one real, unexplained recent swing. Receivable days held in a 7–17 day band for nine of ten years, then spiked to 27.6 days in FY2025 and fully reversed to 9.6 days in FY2026. CAMS carries no inventory, since it is a services business.

Capital allocation: clean by the numbers. A deterministic scan of CAMS's full ten-year record found zero red flags — no runaway receivables, no leverage outpacing earnings, no material dilution, no dependence on other income. CAMS funded a real, growing capital-investment program, repaid debt, and still returned an estimated ~₹291cr to shareholders in FY2026, entirely from its own operating cash flow, with no external financing. The one caveat is the ~₹129.5cr unexplained investing-cash residual discussed above.

Management: a stable, professionally run business with one vague item. Anuj Kumar's re-appointment for a fresh five-year term (from August 2026) suggests continuity. Management referred, in results discussed publicly around FY2025, to a SEBI-related matter as resolved, without disclosing its specific nature — this report's sources could not find further detail. CAMS's FY2026 results carried an unmodified audit opinion, meaning no current audit qualification is on record.

One standing data gap: the ~₹129.5cr unexplained FY2026 investing-cash-flow residual, and the composition of the fast-growing "Other Assets" balance-sheet line. Both would need CAMS's own detailed notes to accounts to resolve fully.

The Story So Far

CAMS in FY2017 was already a well-established, high-margin business, built on a licensed monopoly-like position it shares with just one other firm. Its margins moved with the mutual fund industry's own regulatory cycle from the start: a 2018 SEBI-mandated cut to what funds could charge investors hit CAMS's own margins within the year, and the business recovered within about twelve months as industry assets kept growing regardless.

The five years from FY2021 to FY2025 were CAMS's strongest stretch: revenue more than doubled, operating margin climbed from the low 30s into the low 40s, and the company built up an increasingly generous shareholder-return program funded entirely by its own cash generation, while steadily reducing what little debt-like liability it carries.

FY2026 broke that clean upward run. Revenue kept growing, but only 5.9%, a sharp deceleration from the year before. Operating profit slipped rather than grew, for the first time in the window this report can see, driven by an expense line that more than doubled for reasons not disclosed in enough detail to trace. Receivables swung sharply and then fully reversed within the same year. None of this threatens the balance sheet — cash conversion actually improved to its best level of the decade (132.2%) even in this "wobble" year. But it is a real break in the pattern, sitting right alongside the most significant regulatory change the mutual fund industry has seen since 2018. What happens next depends on whether FY2026 turns out to be a one-year blip, the way FY2019's margin dip was, or the start of something more structural.

The Industry CAMS Competes In, and the Regulatory Backdrop

Where the industry stands. Indian mutual fund industry assets under management stood at roughly ₹73.7 lakh crore (about $790 billion) as of 31 March 2026, having roughly 2.5x'd over the prior five years — a genuinely fast-growing base, still well below what more mature markets typically carry relative to the size of their economies. New-investor growth is, if anything, outpacing even that already-fast industry rate: CAMS's own management disclosed annual SIP (Systematic Investment Plan — a recurring monthly mutual fund investment) registrations of roughly 4.7 crore in FY2026, up 17% year over year and running at "almost 2x of the industry growth," with monthly SIP contributions crossing ₹20,000cr for the first time in March 2026.

New participants are still entering. SEBI was reported, as of mid-2026, to be processing nine new mutual fund license applications. CAMS itself named five new AMCs going live before December 2026, on top of six new mandates already won in FY2026. A market still adding new competitors and new products at this pace is not a mature, saturated one.

The live regulatory event. On 17 December 2025, SEBI approved a full overhaul of mutual fund cost regulations, effective 1 April 2026 — the most significant restructuring of fund-industry fees since the 2018 change discussed above. The new rules replace the old, bundled Total Expense Ratio (the all-in cap on what a fund can charge investors) with a Base Expense Ratio, a narrower figure covering only the AMC's own management fee; other costs like brokerage and exchange fees are now disclosed separately. Some caps were cut outright — for example, index funds and ETFs saw their expense-ratio ceiling reduced from 1.00% to 0.90%. CAMS's own management raised this development unprompted on an earnings call in May 2026, saying the new rule "for some of the large AMCs are underway" with a possibly retrospective effective date, and as of the most recent call reviewed (7 August 2026) said no yield decline had yet materialized in that specific quarter. The rule is only months old — this is genuinely unresolved, not closed out either way.

A smaller, already-realized version of the same risk. CAMS's separate KYC-registration business (KRA) already absorbed a real, disclosed rate cut of roughly 29–30%, effective 1 April 2026, which management directly tied to that segment's margin falling to around 13% in the most recent quarter, against a fuller-year target of 16–17%. This is not a hypothetical stress case — it is a live example, in one of CAMS's own smaller business lines, of exactly the kind of fee compression the larger, unresolved RTA-level question could bring.

Persistent, self-disclosed concentration risk. CAMS's own annual reports, in near-identical language across the years reviewed, state plainly that its revenue is highly concentrated in the mutual fund industry and among a small number of large clients, and that regulatory pressure on fund expense ratios could in turn pressure CAMS's own fees. The company names this risk itself, every year, without ever reporting it as improving.

Cybersecurity. CAMS's most recent annual report names cybersecurity as a standing risk category, given the scale of investor financial data it holds, governed under the ISO 27001:2022 information-security standard. The same report states there were no data breaches or cybersecurity incidents in FY2026 — a clean record to date, though a structural risk that grows as CAMS expands into more data-sensitive lines like account aggregation.

Industry attractiveness for the next 2–3 years: high, tempered by one live variable. A structurally growing, license-protected duopoly, with rising rather than falling barriers to new competition, sitting alongside a genuinely unresolved fee-regulation transition that points directly at the core revenue mechanism.

Macro Backdrop

CAMS is unusually insulated from the macro forces that move most other companies in this report series. Its own FY2026 annual report states directly that it carries no commodity price risk, and separately discloses that its foreign-currency exposure — mostly software licenses and consultancy paid in US dollars — is "limited" and "not material to the size of its operations," with no currency hedging in place. It earns no export revenue and has no cross-border trade exposure.

What does move CAMS is domestic: interest rates, and the tug-of-war between foreign and domestic investors in Indian equity markets. In 2026, foreign investors sold Indian shares at a near-record pace, but Indian mutual funds — fed by the same rising SIP flows discussed above — bought at an even larger scale in at least one month cited (March 2026: mutual funds bought ₹1.05 lakh crore against ₹1.14 lakh crore of foreign selling), keeping the asset base CAMS earns fees on growing despite the foreign outflows. India's own central bank held its policy rate steady at 5.25% through August 2026 with a neutral stance — a stable, non-directional backdrop for now. Separately, both the RBI and the IMF have modestly trimmed their FY2027 India growth forecasts (to 6.6% and 6.4% respectively, from roughly 6.9% and higher previously) — a real, if not severe, deceleration from an already-strong base.

Events that would change this analysis: 1. SEBI's new expense-ratio framework is applied in a way that directly caps or renegotiates RTA-level fees specifically, not just AMC-level costs. 2. Domestic retail SIP flows themselves slow or reverse, removing the one mechanism currently offsetting foreign investor selling. 3. A structural break in the CAMS/KFin duopoly — mass new RTA licensing, or a large AMC building this capability in-house.

Valuation Reality Check

This is where the story turns from "excellent business" to "wait."

Simple multiples. At ₹782 (31 August 2026), CAMS trades at roughly 39.3x trailing earnings on a consolidated basis (Screener.in) — well above the Nifty/Sensex trailing P/E of roughly 19.7x, India's two headline stock-market indices, used here as a stand-in for the broad market. This project's own internal calculation, using CAMS's standalone (rather than consolidated) profit, arrives at a higher 44.7x — the gap exists because CAMS's wholly-owned, profitable subsidiaries add to profit at the consolidated level but not the standalone level used internally elsewhere in this project. Either way, CAMS trades at roughly double the broad market's multiple, and — as shown in the peer table above — at a lower multiple than its only real competitor, KFin Technologies (~49–54x).

The Graham number check. This is a classic value-investing screen: multiply trailing P/E by trailing Price/Book. Benjamin Graham's own rule of thumb treats anything above 22.5 as offering no real margin of safety. Using Screener's consolidated figures (book value ₹53.3/share, implying Price/Book of about 14.7x), P/E × P/B works out to roughly 577 — about 26 times the Graham threshold. This project's own standalone-basis figures (book value ₹48.7/share) put the same calculation at roughly 722, an even larger breach. Either way, this is one of the largest Graham-number breaches found anywhere in this project's coverage so far, larger than this project found for CARE Ratings (≈155) or IEX (≈195).

The reverse-DCF check. Instead of guessing a fair value, this method solves backward from today's market price for the growth rate the market is already pricing in, using a standard five-year-explicit-growth-plus-terminal-value model. Using CAMS's own free cash flow (operating cash flow plus investing cash flow, ₹307cr in FY2026), a company-specific cost of capital of 15.29% (built from a computed Beta of 1.369 — meaning the market treats CAMS's stock as more volatile than the Nifty, a real, computed figure, not an assumption), and a standard 4% terminal-growth assumption: the market's current price implies CAMS's free cash flow needs to compound at 60.2% a year for the next five years.

That number should be read against CAMS's own real growth history. Against the ten-year revenue CAGR (13.2%), the gap is roughly 4.6 times. Against the more recent, faster two-year window (15.7%, FY2024–26), the gap narrows only to roughly 3.8 times. Read plainly: even giving CAMS full credit for its best recent growth stretch, the market is pricing in free cash flow growth almost four times faster than that stretch, sustained for five straight years.

A Note on a Classic Ownership Screen (Pulak Prasad / Nalanda Capital's Framework)

This is a separate, simpler two-number screen from Pulak Prasad and Nalanda Capital's published investing framework: Return on Capital Employed above 20%, and trailing P/E at a 25–30% discount to the Sensex.

  • ROCE check: passes, comfortably. This report's own FY2026 ROCE is 46.2%; Screener's own site shows 47.0%. Either figure clears the 20% bar by a wide margin — among the strongest results this project has found for any company.
  • Valuation-discount check: fails. CAMS trades at roughly double the Sensex's multiple, the opposite of the 25–30% discount this framework calls for.
  • Ownership check: the same structural mismatch this project found for CARE Ratings. This framework prefers founder-led, promoter-owned businesses, or, as a documented exception, listed subsidiaries of global MNC parents. CAMS has 0% promoter ownership today, and its former financial-sponsor owner (Warburg Pincus) was a private equity fund that fully exited, not an operating MNC parent — it fits neither category. This does not automatically make CAMS a worse investment; this particular screen's theory of ownership alignment simply has nothing to say about it either way.

Bull Case, Bear Case, and the Honest Resolution

Bull case. CAMS is the larger, more profitable, and more dominant company in a two-player, license-protected industry, and it trades at a lower earnings multiple than its smaller rival — a genuinely rare combination. Its balance sheet is close to debt-free, self-funding, and has returned an estimated ~₹291cr to shareholders in FY2026 alone while still investing in a real infrastructure build-out. Related-party dealings, checked directly against four years of annual reports, are small and clean. CAMS has weathered a comparable regulatory fee cut before, in 2018, and recovered its margins within about a year. A newly reappointed CEO on a fresh five-year term points to continuity, not disruption. The industry itself is still growing quickly, with new investors and new AMCs continuing to enter.

Bear case. FY2026 already shows two things a bull case has to explain away, not merely acknowledge: an unexplained 135% jump in "Other Expenses" that flipped operating leverage negative for the first time in this record, and roughly ₹129.5cr of investing cash flow this report's sources cannot trace. The one live, unresolved risk in this entire analysis — SEBI's new expense-ratio framework — points directly at CAMS's core fee mechanism, and a smaller, already-realized version of the same risk (the KRA segment's rate cut) is visibly denting that business's margin right now. Even trading cheaper than its rival, CAMS's own reverse-DCF math shows the market is pricing in free cash flow growth nearly four times faster than even its best recent stretch — a bar this business would have to clear for five straight years running, having never quite cleared it even once over any five-year window in its own ten-year record.

Resolution. The deciding factor is whether the realized, full-fiscal-year fee impact of SEBI's new framework — visible only once FY2027 is fully reported — turns out mild (consistent with management's "no yield decline" comments so far) or severe (consistent with the KRA segment's already-realized experience). A second factor: whether FY2026's unexplained expense jump and cash-flow residual turn out to be a one-year event, the way FY2019's margin dip was, or the start of a genuine pattern.

Stress-Testing for a Perfect Storm

This section combines three plausible shocks at once, because that is how real drawdowns actually happen. It uses the FY2027 base-case forecast above as its starting point.

Company-specific shock: margin compression. A further 300-basis-point operating-margin decline, directly extending the real FY2026 event rather than inventing a new one. On FY2027E's base revenue, this cuts operating profit by roughly 7.6%.

Industry shock: SEBI's fee-regulation overhaul bites harder than management currently expects. Modeled as a 10% cut to RTA-level fee yield — anchored conservatively at one-third of the already-realized 29–30% KRA segment rate cut, since core RTA fees are one step removed from that specific revision. This alone cuts revenue by roughly 10%.

Macro shock: a credit-cycle slowdown. RBI rates rise 150 basis points from the current 5.25% neutral stance, working through the same mechanism the macro backdrop above already names — a deceleration in the pace of new money entering mutual funds. Modeled as roughly a 5-percentage-point cut to revenue growth, plus a modest rise in CAMS's own borrowing cost.

Combined effect (avoiding double-counting the two revenue-side shocks, since the regulatory and rate-driven effects act through the same fee-revenue channel): revenue would fall to roughly ₹1,367cr, operating margin to roughly 36.5%, and net profit to roughly ₹398cr — about 8.9% below CAMS's actual FY2026 result, a genuine year-over-year earnings decline, not just a miss against an optimistic forecast.

Survival check under this combined scenario: - Stay cash-flow positive from operations? Yes, comfortably — CAMS's cash conversion has exceeded 100% of profit in most years, including its weakest ones. - Stay below Debt/Equity of 1.5? Yes, trivially — the combined scenario implies a Debt/Equity of roughly 0.036, essentially unchanged from today. - Maintain interest coverage above 2x? Yes, overwhelmingly — roughly 80x covered even under this combined shock. - Retain dividend-paying capacity? Yes — even the stressed ₹398cr profit exceeds what CAMS earned as recently as FY2024 (₹337cr), a year that supported a real dividend program.

Perfect Storm verdict: resilient on solvency, vulnerable on the growth-and-earnings story. CAMS's near-zero real debt and strong cash generation mean this combined scenario poses no threat to the company's survival or its ability to keep paying dividends. What it threatens is the growth story currently priced into the stock — a genuine earnings decline, not just slower growth, in a year the market is currently pricing for something close to the opposite.

The Strongest Case Against Buying This Stock

This section is written as a prosecutor's closing argument, not a balanced summary. Its job is to be at least as forceful as the bull case above.

The core financial vulnerability. CAMS's own most recent year already shows what a bull case has to explain away: profit essentially stalled despite continued revenue growth, driven by a cost line that jumped 135% for reasons this report could not trace, alongside a receivables balance that swung wildly and a cash-flow residual of roughly ₹129.5cr that remains unaccounted for. Three unusual events in one year, still unresolved after every available source was checked.

The risk being underpriced. The market is charging CAMS a valuation multiple that assumes flawless execution through a regulatory transition whose outcome is not yet known. CAMS's own smaller KYC business already shows what a real SEBI-driven rate cut does to a segment's margin — it fell from a 16–17% target to roughly 13% within one quarter of the cut taking effect. The much larger core RTA business sits under the exact same regulator, mid an overhaul management itself has called out on an earnings call, without full clarity on the final outcome. A stock priced at nearly 40 times trailing earnings, and requiring free-cash-flow growth almost four times its own best recent stretch to justify that price, should not be treating this outcome as a low-probability tail risk.

A note on position sizing. Nothing in this report speaks to how large a position in CAMS should be — that is a portfolio-level decision, taken independently of the business-quality findings here. What this report does say is that the valuation and the open questions above are the right inputs to that decision, not any doubt about the quality of the business, which by the numbers here is genuinely strong.

Final Verdict

WAIT. CAMS is, by the numbers in this report, one of the stronger businesses covered in this report series — a dominant, licensed position in a two-player industry, high and genuinely verified returns on capital, a close-to-debt-free balance sheet, and a related-party history checked directly against four years of primary filings and found clean. It is also, right now, cheaper than its only real competitor on a simple earnings-multiple basis, which is not a common combination.

What holds this back from a stronger call is price, layered on top of a real and currently open question mark. Every valuation check in this report — the multiple relative to the broad market, the Graham number, and the reverse-DCF gap even measured against CAMS's own best recent growth — arrives at the same conclusion: the current price already assumes a growth rate this business has not demonstrated over any five-year stretch in its own ten-year record. That assumption is being tested at the exact moment CAMS's most recent full year shows a real, unexplained profitability wobble, sitting alongside a live, unresolved regulatory change aimed squarely at its core fee.

This is not a call to sell a broken business — this report found no such thing. It is a statement that the price leaves very little room for FY2026's open questions to resolve the wrong way.
