What CARE Ratings Does, and Where It Stands Among Its Rivals

A credit rating agency assesses how likely a borrower is to repay a loan or bond on time. It publishes this assessment as a letter grade, from AAA down to D. Lenders and bond investors use this grade instead of doing their own credit assessment.

The company being rated pays the fee. This is called the "issuer-pays" model, used by every rating agency in India, not just CARE. The agency also earns a smaller "surveillance" fee each year the rated debt remains outstanding, since the rating needs periodic review.

This is a licensed business. SEBI, India's securities regulator, decides which agencies may rate debt. As of mid-2026, only nine agencies hold this license nationally. This license is the main barrier to entry in this industry — a new competitor cannot simply start rating debt.

CARE Ratings' legal name is Credit Analysis & Research Ltd. It now operates under the "CareEdge" brand. It was founded in 1993 by a group of public-sector financial institutions — IDBI Bank, Canara Bank, and Unit Trust of India among them — rather than a single promoter.

These founding institutions have since sold their entire stake. As of March 2026, promoter shareholding stands at 0%. CARE Ratings is professionally run, with no controlling owner. This repo's IEX report found the same ownership structure (see the Valuation section for why this matters here too).

Where the Money Went, and One Unusual Balance-Sheet Item

A sources-and-uses check tracks every source of cash against every use, year by year, and confirms the two totals match. For every year checked, CARE's Total Sources minus Total Uses ties out exactly to the company's own reported Net Cash Flow, with zero unexplained residual.

FY2018 is a typical example. ₹163.8cr of sources — operations plus a small investment drawdown — funded ₹153.3cr of uses: a small capex estimate, a bigger increase in investments, and dividends. This matches the company's own reported ₹10.5cr net cash increase for the year.

One balance-sheet line stands out. "Other Assets" grew from ₹57.0cr (FY2017) to ₹890.0cr (FY2026), a roughly 15-fold increase. Over the same period, the separately-labeled "Investments" line shrank, from ₹466.1cr to ₹86.9cr. Cash & Bank balances stayed in a narrower ₹14-129cr range throughout.

The likely explanation is a reclassification. A growing pool of CARE's own investments — likely fixed deposits and similar instruments — may have moved to a different balance-sheet bucket over the years, rather than representing new operating assets. This is not confirmed by the data source available to this report — the same caution this repo applied to a similarly unusual line in its IEX report. What is clear regardless of the bucket: CARE is sitting on a large and growing pool of low-yield liquid assets relative to the size of its core business. This point returns in the capital-allocation discussion below.

The One Piotroski Test CARE Fails, and Why It Doesn't Matter

The Piotroski F-Score, a published year-over-year financial health test explained below, flags CARE for one thing: share count grew every year, and never shrank.

Checked directly: equity share capital moved from ₹29.45cr (FY2017) to ₹30.05cr (FY2026), a cumulative increase of 2.0% over nine years, or about 0.2% a year. This is not a meaningful dilution event. It is almost certainly routine employee stock option exercises — small, ordinary share-count creep that a mechanical test cannot distinguish from an actual capital raise.

How Healthy Is the Business

Revenue quality. CARE's fee income comes from two sources. The first is rating fees tied to specific debt instruments — paid upfront, plus an annual surveillance fee for as long as the rated instrument stays outstanding. The second is a growing non-ratings slice: research, risk analytics, and advisory (through subsidiary CARE Analytics & Advisory Pvt Ltd), and ESG ratings (through CARE ESG Ratings Ltd, which received SEBI approval in 2024). The core ratings business moves with two drivers: how much new corporate debt gets issued each year, and how much bank lending activity generates fresh loan-rating mandates. Both move with the broader credit cycle, which is why revenue fell during FY2019-22, described above.

Profitability trend: decline, then recovery. Operating margin roughly halved, from 62%+ in FY2017-18 to a floor of 29-30% in FY2020-22, then recovered to 38.7% by FY2026 over four straight years of revenue outgrowing costs. Margins remain well below their decade-ago peak. This report found no disclosed plan targeting a specific return to 50%+ margins.

Working capital: clean today, with a resolved historical wobble. Receivable days rose to the 50-60 day range during the FY2019-20 crisis and have held in a tight 25-30 day band for the five years since. CARE carries no inventory, since it is a services business, so manufacturer-style inventory checks do not apply.

Capital allocation: clean by the numbers, with one caveat. A deterministic red-flag scan of CARE's data found zero flags: no runaway receivables, no leverage outpacing earnings, no real dilution, no excessive dependence on other income. The caveat is the "Other Assets" growth discussed above — a large and growing pool of assets appears to sit in low-yield instruments rather than being redeployed into the business or returned to shareholders. The current dividend yield is a modest 1.3%.

Management: clean today, with a resolved historical chapter. Current MD & Group CEO Mehul Pandya has been with CareEdge since 2000 and took charge of the top role on 29 July 2022, for a five-year term. This research found no governance concerns tied to his tenure. Between 2018 and 2026, CARE went through a serious regulatory crisis tied to the IL&FS default, alongside its two main rivals. That episode is covered in full in the next section.

One unresolved data gap. Related-party transaction and loan disclosures — the standard check for promoter fund diversion — are not present in the underlying data source for any company in this report. This would require CARE's actual annual report to check directly. This report does not have that document and does not claim to have verified related-party exposure either way.

The Story So Far

CARE Ratings in FY2017 was a smaller, high-margin agency, generating ₹287.4cr of revenue at 62%+ operating margins, priced on the strength of its regulatory license and reputation, with essentially no debt.

Starting in 2018, the business went through its roughest stretch in this ten-year record. The IL&FS default exposed a genuine industry-wide regulatory failure. A whistleblower complaint and forensic audit implicated CARE's own former Chairman and CEO, and both left the company within months of each other in 2019-2020. Operating margin nearly halved as compliance and review costs rose. Revenue stagnated or declined for three straight years, FY2019-22.

A recovery followed. A new CEO took over in 2020, succeeded by the current CEO in 2022. Revenue compounded at 17.6% a year from the FY2022 low back to ₹473.1cr by FY2026, and operating margin recovered to 38.7%. After roughly seven years of litigation, the individual at the center of the original controversy was fully exonerated by India's securities appellate tribunal, and then by the Supreme Court, as recently as March 2026.

Two things stand out from this cycle. First, cash from operations never went negative, even in the worst year, and the balance sheet never took on real debt. Second, a rating agency is structurally positioned to be blamed after any large borrower default, regardless of what its own data showed at the time. The next credit-cycle downturn could produce a similar story, and the current share price leaves little room for that possibility. That is the subject of the rest of this report.

The Industry CARE Competes In, and the Regulatory Backdrop

Where the industry stands. India's corporate bond market — company-issued debt, which generates a meaningful share of rating-agency fee income — has grown to roughly ₹53.6 lakh crore (about $627 billion) as of early 2026. It has expanded at close to 12% a year over the last decade. CRISIL's own research projects this market to nearly double, to around ₹100 lakh crore, by FY2030.

Even at today's size, India's corporate bond market is only about 15-16% of GDP, against roughly 38% in China, 54% in Malaysia, and 79% in South Korea. This points to a large, multi-decade growth runway ahead, if India's market develops the way those more mature markets already have.

A near-term regulatory tailwind. In 2026, SEBI amended its Credit Rating Agency regulations to let CRAs take on rating assignments regulated by other authorities — the RBI and IRDAI, India's banking and insurance regulators — for the first time. This expands the addressable market beyond the traditional SEBI-regulated debt instruments CRAs have historically covered. New "ring-fencing" rules require separate email IDs, separate website sections, and independently-maintained minimum net worth for this new business line, to keep it separated from SEBI's own regulatory perimeter.

SEBI also standardized what counts as a "sharp rating action" — a ratings drop of three notches or more across two consecutive releases, which must now be specifically flagged — and tightened disclosure requirements around non-cooperating issuers. Both changes respond directly to the kind of failure-to-catch-deterioration criticism the industry faced during the IL&FS episode, described below.

Competitive position. CARE sits in a #2/#3 tier alongside ICRA, both meaningfully smaller than CRISIL (see the comparison table earlier in this report). Nine agencies hold SEBI registration nationally as of mid-2026, but CRISIL, ICRA, and CARE dominate visible market activity. Smaller registered agencies, including India Ratings (a Fitch subsidiary), compete mainly at the margins. CARE holds a genuine, licensed #2/#3 position, protected by the same regulatory-registration moat every player in this industry shares — not a dominant #1 position.

Structural tailwinds: 1. The corporate bond market's multi-year growth trajectory, still early relative to peer economies. 2. SEBI's 2026 expansion of CRA scope into RBI/IRDAI-regulated instruments — a real, new addressable market. 3. CareEdge's own diversification: ESG ratings (SEBI-approved 2024), risk analytics and advisory, and an international push. CareEdge Global IFSC Ltd, based in GIFT City, launched sovereign credit ratings covering 39 countries in October 2024, alongside existing operations in Mauritius, South Africa, and Nepal. This is early-stage, but genuine optionality if it scales. 4. Retail participation in India's bond market has risen steadily through 2025-26, a demand-side tailwind for the whole industry.

Structural headwinds: 1. Bank credit growth — a real driver of bank-loan rating mandates — is forecast by ICRA's own research arm to moderate to roughly 11.0-11.7% for the year ahead. 2. SEBI's broadened scope also opens the door to more competitors, including larger global players, entering segments that were previously closed to everyone. 3. The industry's memory of the IL&FS episode means any future large default, even one CARE plays no direct role in, tends to trigger renewed scrutiny of the whole sector.

Industry attractiveness for the next 3 years: medium-high. A genuine, still-early structural growth runway sits alongside near-term credit-cycle deceleration and a demonstrated pattern of the whole industry getting drawn into blame after large defaults.

The IL&FS Episode — CARE's Most Material Governance History

This is the most important piece of governance history in this report. It is laid out here in full and in chronological order.

Two things are true here, and neither cancels the other out.

First: the industry-wide regulatory failure was real. Three of India's biggest rating agencies, CARE included, kept a AAA rating on a company that was already visibly deteriorating, right up until it defaulted. This was formally penalized and reflects a genuine structural weakness the industry is still working to fix. The 2026 SEBI rule changes on "sharp rating actions" and non-cooperating issuers are a direct regulatory response to this kind of failure.

Second: the specific allegation that CARE's own former CEO personally interfered to protect favorable ratings took nearly seven years to litigate. It ended in March 2026 with the individual fully exonerated at the highest court in the country, and with the regulator's own conduct in pursuing the case criticized in unusually blunt language along the way.

Both facts should be weighed honestly. The systemic rating-quality failure genuinely happened and is a permanent mark on the industry's track record — it is not resolved by Mokashi's personal exoneration. The personal-interference allegation against CARE's former leadership, specifically, did not hold up.

One smaller data point from the same era is worth recording. In September 2019, CARE downgraded Reliance Capital's debt to default status ("D") over what it described as a payment delay. Reliance Capital publicly called the action "pre-meditated and prejudiced," arguing the delay was a technical glitch, not a real default. This dispute falls in the window right after the IL&FS criticism broke. A plausible, unconfirmed read: CARE's post-crisis posture shifted toward flagging deterioration more quickly, the opposite failure mode from the one it was originally criticized for. This report cannot independently judge who was right in that specific dispute, but records it as real, dated context for how CARE behaved in the years right after its own crisis.

Macro Backdrop

The corporate-bond and bank-credit cycle is the dominant macro force on this business. It currently points in two directions. The structural growth story — bond market depth, still well under peer-economy levels — remains intact and is the strongest reason to be constructive on the industry's future. The near-term cyclical signal — ICRA's own forecast of bank credit growth moderating to roughly 11.0-11.7% — is a genuine, if modest, headwind for new mandate volume over the next few quarters.

Net macro position: mixed, positive over the structural horizon, with real near-term deceleration.

Events that would invalidate this analysis: 1. A large corporate or NBFC default in the next 1-3 years where CARE, specifically, is again shown to have been slow to downgrade despite visible warning signs — the same pattern as 2018, recurring under the current management team. 2. Bank credit growth decelerates meaningfully below ICRA's own 11-11.7% base case for more than a couple of quarters. 3. SEBI's broadened CRA scope attracts larger, better-capitalized competitors into CARE's core mid-market segment faster than the bond market itself is growing, compressing fees industry-wide.

What Happens Next: Three Scenarios Through FY2027

Valuation Reality Check

This is where the story turns from "resilient business" to "wait."

Simple multiples. At ₹1,682 (28 August 2026), CARE trades at roughly 28.5x trailing earnings — the Price-to-Earnings ratio, or P/E, is the share price divided by profit per share, and the most common shorthand for how expensive a stock is. This is above ICRA's ~24.2x, below CRISIL's ~39.1x, and well above the Nifty/Sensex trailing P/E of roughly 19.7x, India's two headline stock-market indices, used here as a stand-in for the broad market. CARE trades at a premium to the broad market, not a discount.

The Graham number check. This is a classic value-investing screen: multiply trailing P/E by trailing Price/Book. Benjamin Graham's own rule of thumb treats anything above 22.5 as offering no real margin of safety. CARE's book value per share is roughly ₹310 (total equity ₹932.5cr divided by ~3.01cr shares outstanding), putting Price/Book at roughly 5.4x. P/E × P/B works out to roughly 155, about 7x the Graham threshold. This is less extreme than this report found for IEX (≈195, ~9x the threshold), but still nowhere near a margin of safety.

The reverse-DCF check. Instead of guessing a fair value, this method solves backward from today's market price for the growth rate the market must already be pricing in, using a standard five-year-explicit-growth-plus-terminal-value model. Using CARE's free cash flow (CFO + CFI, an approximation, since the data source has no direct capex line), a company-specific cost of capital of 10.88% (built from a computed Beta of 0.633, meaning the market treats CARE's stock as less volatile than the Nifty, consistent with its low-debt, cash-generative profile), and a standard 4% terminal growth assumption: the market's current price implies CARE's free cash flow needs to compound at 55.4% a year for the next five years.

That number should be read against both growth figures established earlier in this report. Against the ten-year historical revenue CAGR (5.7%), the gap is nearly 10x. Against the more relevant recent trend — the 17.6%-a-year revenue CAGR CARE actually delivered from the FY2022 low through FY2026 — the gap narrows to roughly 3.1x, worse than the 4.5x gap this report found for IEX. Read plainly, this means the market is pricing CARE for a growth rate more than three times its own best recent four-year stretch, sustained for five straight years, even giving the company full credit for its genuine recent recovery.

A Note on a Classic Ownership Screen (Pulak Prasad / Nalanda Capital's Framework)

This is a separate, simpler two-number screen from Pulak Prasad and Nalanda Capital's published investing framework: Return on Capital Employed above 20%, and trailing P/E at a 25-30% discount to the Sensex.

  • ROCE check: passes, narrowly. This report's own FY2026 ROCE is 20.5%, right at the framework's 20% bar. Screener's own site, using a different formula, shows 26.3%. Either way, this clears the threshold, with less room than IEX's 40.5%.
  • Valuation-discount check: fails. CARE trades at a premium to the Sensex, not the 25-30% discount this framework calls for.
  • Ownership check: the same structural mismatch this report found for IEX. This framework prefers founder-led, promoter-owned businesses, or, as a documented exception, listed subsidiaries of global MNC parents. CRISIL and ICRA, majority-owned by S&P Global and Moody's respectively, fit that exception cleanly. CARE Ratings has 0% promoter ownership and no MNC parent — it does not fit either category, for the same reason as IEX: its founding institutional shareholders sold down over time. This does not automatically make it a worse investment. This screen's theory of ownership alignment simply has nothing to say about it either way.

Bull Case, Bear Case, and the Honest Resolution

Bull case. India's corporate bond market is still only 15-16% of GDP, against 38-79% in more developed economies, and CRISIL's own research projects it to nearly double by FY2030. CARE, in a genuine #2/#3 position alongside ICRA, captures a real, licensed share of that growth without needing to unseat a dominant incumbent. The FY2022-26 recovery — 17.6% a year revenue growth, margin climbing from 29% back to 38.7% — shows a business that has already pulled itself out of a multi-year crisis without taking on debt or losing its license. SEBI's 2026 expansion into RBI/IRDAI-regulated instruments, plus CARE's ESG-ratings and international push, add real, early-stage optionality on top of the recovery. The governance overhang that dominated this stock's headlines for years is, as of March 2026, resolved at the highest judicial level.

Bear case. Even giving CARE full credit for its best-ever recent growth stretch, the market is pricing in free-cash-flow growth more than three times faster than that stretch, sustained for five straight years — a bar this business has never once cleared in ten years of data. Operating margin, despite four years of recovery, remains over 2,300 basis points below its FY2017-18 peak, with no disclosed plan to close that gap. Bank-credit growth, a real driver of mandate volume, is itself forecast to decelerate. A rating agency is structurally set up to be blamed disproportionately after the next large corporate default, regardless of what its own data showed at the time — exactly what happened to CARE once already, at a cost of roughly seven years of litigation and two changes of CEO. Nothing in this report's research suggests that structural vulnerability has gone away.

Deadlock check. Both cases are argued with comparable force. The bull case rests on real, verifiable tailwinds — bond-market depth, the SEBI scope expansion, the resolved governance history — that the bear case does not dispute. The bear case rests on real, verifiable numbers — the Graham number, the reverse-DCF gap even against the flattering recent trend, the still-large margin gap to the decade-ago peak — that the bull case does not dispute either. It argues the recovery trajectory more than compensates.

Resolution. The deciding factor is whether CARE's revenue growth over the next two to three years holds closer to its FY2022-26 recovery pace (17.6% a year) or reverts toward its flatter ten-year average (5.7% a year) as bank-credit growth decelerates. A second factor: whether the next credit-cycle downturn produces anything resembling the IL&FS-era pattern of a large rated issuer deteriorating faster than CARE's own ratings kept pace with.

Stress-Testing for a Perfect Storm

This section combines three plausible shocks at once — one company-specific, one industry-level, one macro — because that is how real drawdowns actually happen.

Company-specific shock. A large, high-profile issuer that CARE rates deteriorates and defaults faster than CARE's own rating actions kept pace with — a smaller-scale echo of the 2018 IL&FS pattern. This triggers renewed SEBI scrutiny and, regardless of the eventual legal outcome, immediate reputational damage and client hesitancy.

Industry shock. SEBI's 2026 scope expansion draws in larger, better-capitalized competitors faster than the underlying bond and credit market grows, compressing fee realization across the #2/#3 tier CARE occupies, while leaner competitors compete more aggressively on price.

Macro shock. Bank credit growth decelerates meaningfully below ICRA's 11.0-11.7% base case — a genuine slowdown, not a mild one — reducing new bank-loan rating mandate volume, while corporate bond issuance softens in sympathy. This reproduces conditions close to the FY2019-22 stagnation CARE has already lived through once.

Combined effect. Revenue growth would likely turn flat to negative, against the base case's +13-17%. Operating margin would likely compress back toward the FY2020-22 range (29-32%), as the fixed compliance and analyst cost base spreads over a shrinking or flat revenue base — the same mechanism that drove the original FY2020-22 margin collapse. Net profit could plausibly decline for a multi-year stretch, likely less severe than FY2019-22 given the current management team's post-crisis track record, but similar in shape.

Survival check under this combined scenario: - Remain cash-flow positive from operations? Yes. Cash from operations never went negative even during the actual FY2019-22 crisis. There is no reason a comparable future scenario would be structurally different. - Stay below Debt/Equity of 1.5? Yes, trivially. CARE would need entirely new, large-scale debt to approach this threshold. What little "borrowings" exist today are most likely lease liabilities, not real financial debt. - Maintain interest coverage above 2x? Yes, trivially. FY2026 interest expense of ₹2.5cr against FY2026 EBIT of ₹183.0cr is roughly 73x covered. - Retain dividend-paying capacity? Likely yes. The current payout is modest, roughly 1.3% yield, relative to the large liquid-asset pool discussed earlier.

Perfect Storm verdict: resilient on solvency, vulnerable on reputation and growth. CARE's near-zero real debt and large liquid-asset cushion mean this combined scenario poses essentially no threat to survival or balance-sheet health. The company has, in effect, already run this stress test once, in FY2019-22, and came through it without touching its balance sheet's core strength. What the scenario threatens is the growth-and-reputation story currently priced into the stock. A company already being asked to justify 55.4% a year implied growth has little room to absorb a repeat of its own worst historical stretch without a material re-rating. The mechanism by which rating agencies get blamed after large defaults, demonstrated once already at real cost to this exact company, has not gone away just because the last episode is now legally closed.

The Strongest Case Against Buying This Stock

This section is written as a prosecutor's closing argument, not a balanced summary. Its job is to be at least as forceful as the bull case above.

The core financial vulnerability. CARE's own ten-year record already contains one real example of what happens when its core risk materializes. A governance shock combined with a credit-cycle downturn produced three years of stagnant-to-declining revenue and a near-halving of operating margin. The recovery took four more years to get most, not all, of the way back. The market today is pricing CARE as though that episode is purely historical, with no bearing on the forward path, while asking the stock to deliver a growth rate more than three times faster than its best-ever recent recovery stretch, for the next five years running. Nothing in this report's research found a credible reason the next five years should structurally outperform the last four by that magnitude. A business this exposed to the credit cycle should be valued with more margin for a repeat of its own worst chapter, not less.

The industry risk being underpriced. The market appears to treat the March 2026 Supreme Court vindication of Mokashi as if it closes the book on this company's governance risk generically. It closes the book on one specific individual-level allegation. It does nothing to address the structural reason CARE, and its two main rivals, got into that situation in the first place: rating agencies are paid by the issuers they rate, sit downstream of whatever information those issuers choose to disclose, and get judged after the fact, often severely, sometimes unfairly, whenever a large rated issuer deteriorates faster than the public rating did. SEBI's 2026 rule changes are a direct regulatory acknowledgment that this structural risk is real and ongoing, not resolved.

The investor's dilemma. CARE Ratings is a business this report rates highly on quality. That does not change whether it is a good business — by the numbers in this report, it largely is, with a resolved governance history and a real four-year recovery to show for it. But a valuation disappointment in a position this large has an outsized effect on the whole portfolio, at a time when every independent valuation check in this report — the P/E premium, the Graham number, the reverse-DCF gap even against the most flattering recent growth trend — points the same direction. Sizing decisions are a portfolio-level judgment, not a stock-picking one, and this report does not make that call.

Final Verdict

WAIT. CARE Ratings is a resilient, licensed business that has already lived through, and recovered from, a real multi-year crisis without touching its balance sheet's core strength. Cash from operations stayed positive throughout. Debt never became real. The governance allegation that dominated its headlines for years was, as of March 2026, fully resolved in its former CEO's favor at the highest judicial level in the country. None of that is in question.

What is in question is price. Every valuation check in this report — the P/E premium to the broad market, the Graham number, and the reverse-DCF gap, the last one measured even against this company's most flattering recent growth trend — arrives at the same conclusion: the current price requires a rate of future growth this business has never once demonstrated, and the larger the position taken, the more being wrong about that costs.

This is not a call to sell a fundamentally broken business — this report found no such thing. It is a statement that the current price leaves very little room for the next credit cycle to look anything like the last one did.
