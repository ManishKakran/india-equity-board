Swaraj Engines is a genuinely high-quality, debt-free business — but it is also a single-customer company, priced today for a continuation of the best year the tractor industry has ever had, right after its own forecasters started calling for that year to end.

This is the first standalone report on Swaraj Engines. It is a current holding in this portfolio.

What Swaraj Engines Does

Swaraj Engines makes diesel engines. It sells almost all of them to one company: Mahindra & Mahindra Ltd ("M&M"), which is also its majority owner (52.11% stake). M&M's tractor division builds Swaraj-brand tractors and buys its engines from this company rather than making them in-house.

This is not a normal customer relationship — it is a captive manufacturing arrangement between a parent and its own subsidiary. Swaraj Engines does not compete for orders the way an independent auto-parts supplier would; it exists specifically to supply one division of its own controlling shareholder. That makes it structurally different from most companies in this portfolio: there is no meaningful competitive market-share battle to describe, because Swaraj isn't really competing for customers at all.

What it does have is a direct stake in how well M&M's own tractor business does. M&M is India's largest tractor maker — it recorded a record 43.6% share of the domestic tractor market in the fourth quarter of FY2026 — so Swaraj Engines' growth is, to a very large extent, M&M's growth. That is simultaneously this company's biggest strength (it rides the market leader's own success, with none of the market-share risk an independent competitor carries) and its single biggest risk (it has no other customer to fall back on if that relationship ever changes). Both sides of that trade-off run through this entire report.

Because there is no independent listed competitor doing what Swaraj does, this report does not include a peer-comparison table — building one would mean comparing Swaraj against companies it isn't actually competing with.

A Cash-Flow Reconciliation Gap — Verified, and Corrected From the First Draft

The first automated pass through this data flagged something that looked alarming: in FY2026, the cash flow statement reports a small net increase in cash (+₹10.70cr), but the balance sheet's own Cash & Bank line actually fell by ₹52.47cr — a ₹63.17cr gap between what the two statements say happened to the company's cash. That first draft treated this as the single most important open question in the whole report, specific to FY2026.

Before finalizing this report, that claim was checked against the full 9-year record (FY2018–FY2026) this repo holds for the company, not just the 3 years the automated draft had access to. The gap is real, but it is not new, and it is not specific to FY2026 — it shows up in 8 of the last 9 years, ranging from ₹2cr to as much as ₹66cr (FY2024 actually had a larger gap than FY2026), switching sign back and forth with no consistent pattern:

A gap this persistent, in both directions, across nine separate years, is much more consistent with a definitional difference — most likely, some portion of the company's fixed deposits or similar short-term instruments getting classified as "cash equivalents" on the cash flow statement in some years and as "Investments" or a similar balance-sheet line in others, depending on their remaining maturity at each year-end — than with an operational cash-flow problem specific to FY2026. This repo's own sources-and-uses reconstruction of Swaraj's cash flows, built independently from the raw balance-sheet and P&L data, also confirms the underlying cash-flow-statement figures (CFO + CFI + CFF) tie out exactly to the statement's own reported net change every single year, with zero unexplained residual — the mismatch sits specifically between the statement's number and the balance sheet's Cash & Bank line, not inside the cash flow statement itself.

This finding is downgraded, not dismissed. It remains a real reason this company's cash figures can't be taken at perfect face value in any single year — but it is a long-standing, structural quirk in how this data source presents the company's cash position, not a fresh, FY2026-specific red flag. The rest of this report treats it accordingly.

Every single year checked — FY2022 through FY2026 — shows essentially 100% of trade receivables owed by M&M, and the rupee figures reconcile almost to the decimal against this company's own balance sheet. This is not "most" of the business sitting with one related party; it is, functionally, the entire receivables base and close to the entire revenue base, every year, for at least five years running. Money owed to suppliers by Swaraj, in the other direction, is tiny (₹0.37–0.77cr) — confirming that the "Other Liabilities" growth visible in the raw numbers above is not related-party-driven.

The governance process around this relationship is disclosed and appears procedurally sound: shareholders approved a ₹2,000cr/year related-party transaction cap with M&M in 2018, raised to ₹5,000cr/year through FY2027-28 in 2023 specifically to accommodate expected volume growth; the auditors flag these transactions as a Key Audit Matter every year (standard practice for a relationship this size, not itself a red flag); and directors with a personal interest are recorded as excluded from voting on the relevant board resolutions. None of this changes the underlying fact — it only confirms the process around it follows the required disclosure rules.

What this data source cannot show, in either direction, is the actual pricing: whether Swaraj sells engines to M&M on arm's-length terms comparable to what an independent supplier would charge, or on terms that favor the parent. No annual report reviewed discloses a benchmarking study or independent fairness assessment of the pricing itself.

How Healthy Is the Business

Revenue quality. Growth has been almost entirely volume-driven — engine unit sales and revenue have moved in near-lockstep for the one year this data can check directly (FY2026: +20.1% units vs. +19.34% revenue), meaning the company isn't relying on price increases to grow. Over the most recent two years, revenue compounded at roughly 19% a year, meaningfully faster than its own longer-run 13.0% historical average — growth has genuinely accelerated recently, which matters both for how the numbers below are read and for how much confidence to place in that pace continuing.

Profitability: improving, but decelerating. Operating margin has improved for three straight years (12.04% → 12.31% → 12.44%), though the size of each year's improvement has been shrinking (+27 basis points, then +13). Profit still grew faster than revenue in both years (positive operating leverage), but that gap also narrowed in FY2026. One thing this data cannot explain: profit grew slightly slower than operating profit in FY2026 (18.27% vs. 20.63%, a gap that widened from the year before), and neither the tax rate nor interest expense — both essentially flat — accounts for it.

Working capital: a real but explained deterioration. Receivable days rose from 31 to roughly 38 between FY2024 and FY2025/26, and stayed there rather than reversing. Given the related-party data above, this is very likely a function of the credit terms extended specifically to M&M, not a general collection-quality problem with other customers — because there effectively are no other customers. It is still worth watching, since a further rise would tie up more cash in this one relationship.

Capital allocation: cash cow, with a real capex step-up underway. The dominant, consistent use of cash across all three years is dividends — ₹112–128cr a year, rising every year, against a company carrying almost no debt. On top of that, FY2026 saw a real, self-funded capacity expansion begin (from 1,95,000 to 2,40,000 units/year of capacity), reflected in a jump in capital-work-in-progress. This is not a debt-funded pivot or a cash-burn phase — it's an already-profitable business layering a modest growth investment on top of its existing shareholder-payout habit.

Management: clean on every checkable dimension except one. Tax payout has stayed in a tight 25.47–25.58% band for three years, with no volatility suggestive of manipulation. Share count has not moved at all in three years — zero dilution. Dividends have risen every year without interruption. The one dimension this report cannot give a clean pass on is the related-party pricing question above: the process around it is sound, but the actual commercial terms are not independently verifiable from any data available to this report.

The Story So Far

Swaraj Engines closed FY2026 with revenue of ₹2,007cr, up 19% from ₹1,682cr the year before, continuing a pattern of steady, largely volume-driven growth. Profit after tax rose 18% to ₹196cr, and operating margin improved for a third straight year to 12.44%, showing the company converted growing volumes into slightly better profitability rather than simply riding higher prices. The balance sheet carried almost no debt — borrowings of just ₹2cr against nearly ₹489cr of equity — and the share count stayed unchanged across all three years, so growth came without diluting existing shareholders. The company also paid out cash consistently, and in growing amounts, every year.

That said, FY2026's cash flow told a more complicated story than its profit-and-loss statement did. Operating cash flow fell by a third even as profit grew, mostly traced to an unexplained rise in a broad "Other Assets" line this data source doesn't break down further. Receivables had also jumped sharply the year before, only partly settling down since. And running beneath all of it sits one structural fact, confirmed independently across five years of the company's own annual reports: its controlling shareholder is also, by the nature of its business, its only real customer — a relationship whose actual pricing terms are not disclosed anywhere in the numbers reviewed for this report.

The Industry, and Where Swaraj Sits In It

Cycle position: at, or just past, the peak. India's domestic tractor industry sold a record 11,60,300 units in FY2026, up 23% — the first time it has ever crossed one million units, let alone this. Independent forecasters are now guiding a sharp slowdown for FY2027: ICRA projects 1–4% growth, Crisil projects 0–2%, both well below FY2026's pace. This deceleration is already showing up in the most recent data available — April–July FY2027 wholesale volumes grew 19% year-on-year, still positive but visibly slower than FY2026's full-year 23%.

Competitive position: strong, with one recent wrinkle. Comparing Swaraj's own engine dispatches against total industry volume, year by year, using the company's own disclosed figures from five annual reports:

Swaraj outgrew the broader tractor industry in four of the last five years, often by a wide margin. FY2026 is the first year in this five-year window where it grew slightly slower than the industry as a whole — a small reversal, and one this data cannot fully explain. Live research confirms M&M itself gained market share in FY2026 (a record 43.6% in the fourth quarter), so the shortfall doesn't appear to be M&M losing ground generally — it may reflect something specific to the Swaraj-branded tractor line versus M&M's other tractor platforms, which this data doesn't break out separately. This is flagged as an open question, not a resolved one.

Tailwinds: 1. A real, already-realized government tax cut: GST on tractors and tractor engines/components was cut from 12% to 5% effective 22 September 2025, saving farmers ₹41,000 to over ₹1,00,000 per tractor depending on horsepower — already visible in a 45% year-on-year sales spike the following month. 2. Structural farm mechanization and a shrinking rural labour pool — cited consistently across five years of the company's own reports as the core long-term demand driver, independent of any single year's weather. 3. A new emission-norm standard (TREM V) has been deferred to 2032 for the horsepower range Swaraj's engines fall into — no near-term compliance disruption over the next three years. 4. M&M's own continued market leadership and recent share gains support underlying demand for Swaraj-built engines specifically.

Headwinds: 1. The FY2027 growth deceleration described above — already forecast, and already partly visible in the data. 2. The 2026 southwest monsoon started below-normal (an early-season forecast tied to El Niño conditions), though the shortfall has been narrowing through the season — not yet fully resolved as of this report. 3. Electric tractors are a real, if still very early, long-term threat to diesel-engine demand — negligible today (well under 100 registered nationally, against roughly 9 million diesel tractors on the road), but industry estimates put them at 5–7% of annual sales by 2027. 4. Total, undiversified dependence on one customer — confirmed above across five years of primary-source data. No diversification plan of any kind has been disclosed in any of the five annual reports reviewed, and none of them names customer concentration as a risk in its own right — the company frames its risk purely as general exposure to the tractor industry.

Industry attractiveness for the next 3 years: medium. A large, structurally growing industry with real government support sits alongside a business that just came off its best year ever, with forecasters already calling for a sharp slowdown and a slow-moving disruption risk on the horizon. Not a clean "high."

Macro Backdrop

Two things outside Swaraj's control matter more than almost anything else here: the price of steel, and the monsoon.

Raw materials — mostly steel castings — made up roughly 78% of FY2026 revenue, by far the company's largest cost line. The current stretch of cheap steel is a real tailwind, but it is a byproduct of China's own manufacturing oversupply, not anything this company controls or earned. If China launches a large enough stimulus program to absorb that oversupply, steel prices could reverse quickly, and this company does no commodity hedging of any kind — any spike would pass through directly. This is, by a wide margin, the single most important variable to watch on the cost side.

On the demand side, the 2026 monsoon started with a below-normal forecast but the shortfall has narrowed meaningfully through the season (from roughly 30% below normal in June to roughly 12% by mid-August) — improving, but not yet fully resolved as of this report. A weak final outturn would hit rural income and, with it, tractor demand directly.

The company carries essentially no foreign-currency risk: its FY2025-26 annual report states directly that the business has no significant foreign exchange exposure and does no hedging, and confirms zero export revenue. The only meaningful foreign-currency line is import spending on capital equipment for the ongoing capacity expansion (₹26.44cr in FY2026, up from ₹4.75cr the year before) — capex-related, not a recurring cost exposure. This also means the company is shielded from currency and global interest-rate swings that hit some of this portfolio's other holdings harder.

Domestic interest rates matter through a different, smaller channel: many tractor purchases are financed, so the RBI's repo rate affects EMI affordability for the end buyer (not Swaraj's own borrowing cost, which is negligible). The rate currently sits at 5.25%, near the low end of its range over the last five years — a supportive-to-neutral backdrop for now.

One scenario worth watching closely is a genuine Chinese stimulus pushing steel prices back up at the same time a weak monsoon squeezes rural tractor demand — that combination, not either shock alone, is where the real near-term risk concentrates.

What to watch each quarter: domestic tractor-industry wholesale volume against the ICRA/Crisil guided range; Swaraj's own dispatch growth against M&M's Swaraj-brand tractor sales specifically (watching whether FY2026's first-ever underperformance reverses or persists); the monsoon's progress and then actual rural-income data; domestic and Chinese steel-price trends; receivable days each quarter; whether the cash-flow-reconciliation pattern discussed earlier ever gets a direct explanation in a future annual report; RBI policy decisions; and any disclosure hinting at a change in M&M's sourcing strategy.

Valuation Reality Check

This is where the story turns from "strong business" to "wait."

Simple multiples. At ₹3,557.8 (4 September 2026), Swaraj trades at roughly 21.4x trailing earnings — well above the Nifty/Sensex trailing P/E of roughly 19.7x, and near the top end of its own recent range. Book value per share works out to roughly ₹402 (FY2026 total equity of ₹488.92cr ÷ 1.215cr shares), putting Price/Book at roughly 8.8x.

The Graham number check. A classic value-investing screen: multiply trailing P/E by trailing Price/Book. Benjamin Graham's own rule of thumb treats anything above 22.5 as offering no real margin of safety. Swaraj's figure works out to roughly 189 — more than 8 times that threshold. This is a common outcome for a business this profitable and this capital-light (the same screen penalizes any high-ROCE, debt-free compounder structurally), but it is nowhere near a margin of safety by this specific measure.

The reverse-DCF check. Rather than guessing a fair value, this method solves backward from today's market price for the growth rate the market must already be pricing in, using a standard five-year-growth-plus-terminal-value model. Using this company's own free cash flow, a company-specific cost of capital of 15.6% (built from a computed Beta of 1.41 — meaning the market treats this stock as meaningfully more volatile than the Nifty, consistent with its cyclical, industry-linked profile), and a standard 4% terminal growth assumption: the market's current price implies free cash flow needs to compound at 38.1% a year for the next five years.

That figure should be read against this company's own growth history. Against the 13.0% long-run historical revenue CAGR, the gap is close to 3x. Even against the faster, more flattering recent 2-year growth rate of roughly 19% — the pace the company has actually delivered in its best recent stretch — the market's implied number is still roughly double. Read plainly: the current price assumes a growth rate well above anything this company has demonstrated, sustained for five straight years.

A Note on a Classic Ownership Screen (Pulak Prasad / Nalanda Capital's Framework)

A separate, simpler two-number screen from Pulak Prasad and Nalanda Capital's published investing framework: Return on Capital Employed above 20%, and trailing P/E at a 25–30% discount to the broad market.

  • ROCE check: passes, comfortably. This company's ROCE runs roughly 48–55% depending on the year — well clear of the 20% bar.
  • Valuation-discount check: fails. The stock trades at a premium to the Sensex, not the 25–30% discount this framework calls for.
  • Ownership check: passes cleanly. M&M holds 52.11% of Swaraj Engines directly, a genuine, engaged controlling promoter with every incentive to see the business run well — exactly the ownership structure this framework prefers, unlike some of this portfolio's other holdings with no promoter at all.

Bull Case, Bear Case, and the Honest Resolution

Bull case. Swaraj is a debt-free, roughly-50%-ROCE claim on India's largest tractor maker's own growth, with a disclosed, auditor-scrutinized related-party framework and zero equity dilution across the tracked period. A realized GST cut, a currently-benign steel-cost environment, and M&M's own recent market-share gains are all live tailwinds working today, not hoped-for future catalysts. Even in a slower FY2027, the company's asset-light, self-funded capex model means shareholders keep collecting a rising dividend regardless of how fast the cycle moves.

Bear case. Swaraj has no revenue outside the one related party that owns it, has disclosed no diversification plan in five years of annual reports, and the one year its dispatch growth actually lagged the broader industry — FY2026 — is the most recent year on record, precisely when a captive supplier should be most insulated, not least. The stock trades at roughly 21x trailing earnings with a Graham ratio nearly 8 times the classic value threshold, priced for continuation of a record year its own cited forecasters say is about to decelerate sharply. And FY2026's operating cash flow fell by a third even as profit rose, an unexplained gap sitting directly beneath a stock priced for a clean story.

Resolution. Whether Swaraj's engine dispatches keep tracking — or resume outpacing — M&M's own Swaraj-brand tractor sales through the FY2027 slowdown will decide which case wins, since that one relationship is simultaneously the entire foundation of the bull case and the central risk of the bear case.

The Master Investor's Story

Swaraj Engines is not a company competing for tractor buyers — it is the engine room, literally, sitting one layer beneath Mahindra & Mahindra's own tractor business. Five years of its annual reports tell the same story every time: almost every rupee of revenue, and every rupee sitting in receivables, traces back to one counterparty, its own majority owner. That is an unusual structure to underwrite, and yet it has produced a genuinely clean set of numbers through FY2026 — revenue and profit both growing near 19–20%, margins improving for a third straight year, no debt to speak of, and not a single new share issued.

The tide it is sailing in crested this year. FY2026 was the tractor industry's best year ever, over a million units sold for the first time, and Swaraj rode that wave — until, oddly, the very last stretch of it, when its own dispatch growth fell just behind the industry's for the first time in five years. Whether that is noise or the first sign of something is not yet knowable. The weather around it has been kind lately too: a government tax cut landed right in the middle of the year, and steel — nearly four-fifths of every rupee this company spends — has stayed cheap only because China is oversupplying the world, not because anything here earned that discount.

What is exciting is the arithmetic: a business this asset-light, compounding capital at something like 50% returns, funding its own expansion out of cash flow, paying rising dividends the whole way through. What is concerning is everything sitting just beneath that arithmetic — receivables that crept up and never fully came back down, and a customer-concentration risk so total that the company's own annual reports never once name it as a risk in their own words. If there is one number worth watching over the next two years, it is not revenue growth or margin — it is whether Swaraj's dispatches keep pace with M&M's own tractor sales, because that is the thread everything else is tied to.

Stress-Testing for a Perfect Storm

This section combines the most plausible company-level, industry-level, and macro shocks at once, applied against the FY2027 base-case forecast above (revenue ₹2,087cr, operating margin 12.55%, profit ₹204cr) — because that is closer to how real drawdowns actually happen than testing each risk in isolation.

Company-specific shock: margin compression. A genuine Chinese fiscal stimulus reverses today's cheap-steel environment — the single largest identified risk on the cost side, since raw materials run roughly 78% of revenue and this company hedges none of that exposure. Modeled as a 300-basis-point margin hit, holding revenue flat: profit falls from ₹204cr to roughly ₹155cr, a 24% decline.

Industry shock: a genuine sector downcycle. Rather than the already-forecast 1–4% deceleration, industry volume falls outright by roughly 10% — a scale seen twice before in this company's own five-year record. Applying the company's own historical pattern of gaining share even in down years softens the blow to roughly a 5% revenue decline: profit falls to roughly ₹193cr, a 5% decline.

Macro shock: a domestic interest-rate spike. The RBI raises rates by 200 basis points (5.25% → 7.25%), denting tractor-financing affordability for end buyers. Using a judgment-call estimate (no directly computable elasticity exists in this data) of roughly a 4% incremental revenue hit: profit falls to roughly ₹195cr, a 4% decline.

Combined effect. The sector downcycle and the rate spike both act on the same underlying driver — rural purchasing power — so they are not simply added together; a dampened combined revenue effect of roughly −7% is used instead. The margin-compression shock acts on a separate axis (input cost, not demand) and is layered on top of that.

Can the company survive this? Yes, comfortably. It would remain solidly cash-flow positive (~₹123cr), its debt-to-equity ratio would barely move given how little debt exists to begin with, interest coverage would stay overwhelming (400x+), and dividend-paying capacity would be retained, just at a reduced level. Even combining the two most-feared risks identified in this report — a demand-side shock and a cost-side shock — this company's near-zero-debt, asset-light structure means an operating shock of this size simply cannot create a survival problem.

The real unresolved risk this stress test does not capture is not cyclical at all — it is the structural one: a partial or full loss of the M&M relationship itself. That scenario was deliberately left out of this combination as a distinct, lower-probability risk (see the thesis-breakers list above), not because it is less severe — it is more severe — but because nothing in this research signals it is imminent enough to model alongside the cyclical shocks above.

The Strongest Case Against Buying This Stock

This section is written as a prosecutor's closing argument, not a balanced summary. Its job is to be at least as forceful as the bull case above.

The core financial vulnerability. This company's FY2026 profit grew 18% while its actual operating cash flow fell by a third — a genuine divergence between paper profit and cash profit in the same year the stock is being asked to justify a rich multiple. Layer on a related-party structure where essentially the entire revenue base sits with the controlling shareholder, on pricing terms that are not independently verifiable from any data available to this report, and there are numbers here that cannot be fully trusted on their own terms without the company's own primary-source annual report going further than it currently does.

The industry risk being underpriced. The market is pricing this stock at nearly nine times the classic Graham value ceiling, betting on continuation of a record year its own cited forecasters say is ending, propped up by a Chinese oversupply glut that could reverse the moment Beijing chooses to stimulate — a single external lever, entirely outside management's control, sitting under roughly 78% of this company's costs. And the one year in five that this company's own growth actually lagged the industry it serves is the most recent one on record — right when a captive, insulated supplier should have been least exposed to that kind of miss, not most.

What would change this view. None of this alone kills the thesis — a debt-free, ~50%-ROCE business earns some premium, and this report's own hard checks all came back clear. But if the FY2027 annual report shows the cash-flow-reconciliation pattern behaving unusually relative to its own historical range, or if Swaraj's dispatch growth falls meaningfully further behind M&M's Swaraj-brand tractor sales for a second year running, both of the two most bull-supporting assumptions in this report would need to be revisited.

Final Verdict

WAIT. Swaraj Engines is, by nearly every operating measure in this report, a genuinely excellent business: debt-free, extraordinarily capital-efficient, run by a controlling shareholder with a direct and obvious interest in seeing it succeed, and consistent about returning cash to shareholders without ever diluting them. None of that is in question.

What is in question is price and timing. The stock trades at a rich multiple by every valuation check run here — a Graham ratio roughly 8 times the classic threshold, and a reverse-DCF implied growth rate roughly double even this company's own best recent stretch — at the exact moment its own industry has just posted its best year ever and independent forecasters are calling for that to end. Add to that a total, undiversified dependence on a single related party whose actual pricing terms cannot be independently confirmed, and the case for paying up further here, rather than simply holding what is already owned, is weak.

This is not a call to sell a fundamentally sound business — this report found no such thing. It is a statement that the current price leaves very little room for the next year of the tractor cycle to look anything other than exactly as good as the forecasters currently expect.
