PART 1: INDUSTRY OUTSIDE VIEW (Convergence / Gate 2 Pre-Screen)

Sector Overview — Indian IT Services & Software Consulting

The Indian IT services and software consulting industry is the backbone of India's export economy, contributing 7.3% of GDP, employing ~5.8 million professionals, and generating $283 billion in revenue in FY2025 (5.1% YoY growth). The sector accounts for roughly 43–45% of total services exports.

Gartner (Nov 2025) projects India's total IT spending to reach $176.3 billion in 2026 (+10.6%), with software spending alone surging 17.6% to $24.7 billion driven by AI-enabled applications. IT services spending is forecast to grow 11.1% in 2026, with double-digit growth averaging 12–14% over the next few years.

2026 Macro Landscape: Cautious Optimism with AI as the Pivotal Theme

The sector heads into FY27 with three structural tailwinds and three structural headwinds:

Tailwinds: - Enterprise AI adoption accelerating from experimentation → at-scale deployment. Agentic AI is replacing Gen-AI pilots. Gartner estimates 40% of enterprise applications will integrate task-specific AI agents by end of 2026. - Deal pipelines remain resilient. Tier-1 firms reported record/near-record TCV in FY26 (TCS: $40.7 bn, Infosys: $14.9 bn large deals — 28% YoY growth). - Domestic IT spending outpacing export growth; GCC (Global Capability Centre) expansion in India accelerating.

Headwinds: - AI-led productivity compression: clients are extracting more output per dollar of IT spend, compressing volumes even as deal values hold. - 60% revenue concentration in the US market — tariff uncertainty and US macro VUCA (Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, Ambiguity) environment. - Structural workforce reset: TCS reduced headcount by 23,460 in FY26; the linear "headcount = revenue" model is broken.

PART 6: SECTOR-LEVEL DARWIN RISKS TO MONITOR

The AI Disruption Paradox (Filter 1.5 at Sector Level)

The Darwin framework flags "fast-changing industries" as dangerous. The IT services sector is undergoing its most significant structural change since the cloud migration of 2010–2015. The key question is: Are incumbents being disrupted or are they the disruptors?

Evidence suggests the Tier-1 and quality mid-tier firms are enabling AI, not being displaced by it: - TCS: $2.3B AI revenue, $40.7B TCV in FY26 - Infosys: $14.9B large deals (+28% YoY), 55% net-new - Persistent: 22 consecutive quarters of growth, AI-led engineering platform - KPIT: Automotive software growing 15.9% YoY in a challenging auto environment

The risk is compression, not elimination. AI productivity tools mean clients get more output per rupee of IT spend — this compresses revenue per engineer even as deal values hold. Companies that adapt to outcome-based, platform-led models (not headcount-led) will survive and compound. Those that don't will face ROCE compression.

Monitor quarterly: TCS AI revenue disclosure, Infosys large deal net-new percentage, and any sign of pricing pressure in contract renewals.

US Concentration Risk

60% of Indian IT export revenue is US-derived. US tariff volatility, immigration (H-1B visa) restrictions, and macro slowdown are ongoing proximate risks. Darwin framework tells us to focus on ultimate causes — the structural stickiness of IT service relationships (multi-year contracts, deep integration) means this risk is more volatility than permanent impairment for quality names.

PART 14: DARWIN INSIGHTS FROM THE 101–105 ANALYSIS

What Companies 101–105 Confirm About Micro-Cap IT

1. The tail of the listed universe is a value graveyard. All five companies in S.No. 101–105 are eliminated before Gate 1 is even formally evaluated. This is not surprising — it is consistent with Darwin's observation that only the fittest survive, and the vast majority of organisms (companies) do not. The Indian micro-cap IT space below ₹10 Cr market cap is populated almost exclusively by companies that were unable to compete, adapted poorly to technology shifts, or never achieved viable scale.

2. The promoter abandonment signal (Quintegra) is the clearest red flag in markets. When promoters of a 30-year-old company hold only 2.48% of their own business, they have voted with their equity. Darwin's Filter 1.1 and 1.6 together make this a zero-analysis rejection. No fundamental analysis is needed when the founders themselves have exited.

3. Auditor resignations in micro-caps are always serious. Both Jointeca Education and Adjia Technologies had auditor changes/resignations in 2025-26. For a company with ₹24 lakh in half-year revenue (Adjia), an auditor resigning over fee disagreements is an existential governance concern — not a procedural footnote.

4. Business model pivots at this scale almost never work. Sparc Electrex pivoting from IT security software to electrical tools manufacturing (renaming itself in 2022) while losing money across both iterations is a textbook example of a company searching for a viable identity 35 years after founding. The Darwin framework correctly identifies this as a turnaround that has never turned.

5. Serial acquirers from the 2000s Indian IT boom are now shells. Quintegra's aggressive acquisition spree of 2003–2008 (5 deals in 5 years, including US and Ireland subsidiaries) resulted in negative book equity and a ₹5 Cr market cap 15–18 years later. This precisely validates Pulak Prasad's Filter 1.4: "We know we can't price the risk" of serial acquirers.

6. The AR/VR niche requires exceptional scrutiny on Filter 1.5. Adjia Technologies' business in enterprise AR/VR is among the fastest-changing technology niches globally. Meta's continued iteration, Apple Vision Pro's tepid enterprise adoption, and Microsoft HoloLens's discontinuation (2023) demonstrate exactly the "rulebook rewriting every 3–5 years" condition that Darwin's framework warns against. Even if Adjia's management were excellent and the business were profitable, Filter 1.5 would still require careful monitoring.
