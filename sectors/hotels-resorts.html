🏨 Hotels & Resorts Industry — Darwin Investor Analysis

Framework: What I Learned About Investing from Darwin (Pulak Prasad / Nalanda Capital) applied to the NSE/BSE-listed Indian hospitality universe (50 companies)

Date of analysis: June 23, 2026 | Data as supplied + live market research

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Of the 50 listed hotel companies in the supplied universe, only 6 clear Nalanda's non-negotiable first hurdle — historical ROCE >20%. Of those 6, one is a Government of India PSU (automatic disqualification), one carries an unresolved insolvency/NCLT history, and one is sitting on an active SEBI regulatory-censure and failed-delisting situation. That leaves a working shortlist of just four names — EIH Ltd, EIH Associated Hotels, Benares Hotels, and Sayaji Hotels (Pune) — that survive Gate 1 and proceed to quality/valuation analysis. None of them currently trades at the 25–30% discount to the index that Nalanda requires to pull the trigger. The honest, book-disciplined verdict for the entire industry today is:

No BUY NOW. Three WATCHLIST names (EIH, EIH Associated Hotels, Sayaji Hotels Pune — each with caveats). One MONITOR (UP Hotels, pending regulatory resolution). 45 AVOID/DISQUALIFY.

This is not a pessimistic call — it is what the framework is built to produce. Hotels are real-asset, capital-intensive, cyclical businesses; Nalanda's own playbook would expect a thin shortlist here, and the data confirms it.

PART 1 — INDUSTRY OVERVIEW & LATEST DEVELOPMENTS (FY2026)

Where the cycle stands (ICRA, January 2026 update): Indian hospitality is in its strongest multi-year stretch since before the 2008 financial crisis. ICRA expects industry revenue to grow 9–12% in FY2026 even against a high FY2025 base, with pan-India premium hotel occupancy holding at 72–74% and average room rates (ARRs) rising to ₹8,200–8,500. Cost discipline carried over from the pandemic years has pushed operating margins for ICRA's sample of 13 large hotel entities to 34–36% in FY2026 — nearly double the 20–22% margins typical of the pre-COVID era. The structural driver is a persistent demand-supply gap: premium room demand is growing 8–9% a year while new premium room supply across India's 12 key cities is growing only 5–6% CAGR through FY2028. ICRA expects this imbalance, and the pricing power it gives existing hotel owners, to persist for at least another 2–3 years.

January 2026 also brought a reminder that the cycle is not immune to shocks: travel sentiment was dented earlier in FY2026 by terror-related disruptions and geopolitical developments, and flight-duty-time-limitation (FDTL) rules caused flight cancellations in December 2025 that briefly hit business travel. Both proved temporary — long-weekend and post-holiday leisure demand pulled occupancy and ARR back onto trend by January 2026 — but they are a useful real-world illustration of "proximate" shocks that the Darwin framework explicitly tells investors to look through rather than react to.

Structural growth drivers: - Rising discretionary income and a growing aspirational middle class — India's hospitality leaders increasingly frame the opportunity as serving "the next 500 million" middle-income travellers, not just scaling existing demand. - Government infrastructure push: airport expansion, highway connectivity, and "infra status" recently extended to 50 tourist destinations. - Religious/heritage tourism corridors (e.g., the Kashi Vishwanath corridor in Varanasi) creating durable footfall growth in specific cities — directly relevant to single-city operators like Benares Hotels. - MICE (meetings, incentives, conferences, events), weddings, and domestic leisure travel are now structural demand pillars, not just business-travel recovery. - Balance sheets across the large-hotel cohort have strengthened materially on the back of higher cash accruals — debt coverage metrics are comfortable industry-wide (ICRA).

Key risks to underwrite: - The current margin and ROCE levels are running well above historical/pre-COVID norms. A chunk of today's elevated profitability is a cyclical supply-demand mismatch, not a permanent re-rating of unit economics — new room supply is in the pipeline and will eventually catch up. - Geopolitical/security shocks (seen twice in the last 12 months) can dent demand for a quarter at a time. - A meaningful part of the industry (the smid-cap and micro-cap tail in this dataset) is highly fragmented, debt-burdened, or loss-making — this is not a uniformly attractive industry; it is a "some companies make money" industry (see Part 4). - Online travel aggregators and the rise of alternative/home-stay accommodation are a slow-moving but real disintermediation risk for smaller, less-branded operators.

(Source: ICRA Hospitality Industry sector updates, January 2026 and June 2025; Asian Hospitality/CII commentary, June 2025)

PART 2 — METHODOLOGY NOTE (read before the verdicts)

The framework formally runs Gate 1 (six qualitative risk filters) before Gate 2 (the quantitative ROCE/quality/valuation tests). With 50 companies, running full promoter-integrity and litigation research on every name before any quantitative filtering is impractical — and unnecessary, because the book itself describes Nalanda's actual practice as starting with the ROCE screen: "Our preliminary short list of about 150 companies consists only of those that have delivered ROCE of more than 20 percent." This report therefore:

  1. Runs the ROCE >20% screen (Analysis 2.1) across all 50 companies first, exactly as Nalanda does to build its own shortlist universe.
  2. Applies the full six-filter Gate 1 only to the companies that pass (or narrowly miss) that screen.
  3. Carries Gate-1 survivors into full Gate 2 (robustness, management, valuation, proximate-vs-ultimate) and Gate 3 (permanent ownership) analysis.
  4. Gives every other company an AVOID verdict with the specific reason, rather than a full write-up — consistent with the framework's instruction to stop immediately on a disqualifying fact.

Important data caveat: The ROCE, ROE, and FCF figures supplied are point-in-time / trailing figures, not the full 5–10 year time series the book ideally wants. Current-cycle ROCE for the entire hotel industry is inflated by the post-COVID demand-supply imbalance described in Part 1 (industry-wide margins are ~35% today vs ~20–22% pre-COVID). Wherever possible this report cross-checks against multi-year trend data pulled from company disclosures, but readers should pull each shortlisted company's full 10-year ROCE history from its annual reports before sizing any position — exactly as the book recommends.

PART 4 — THE CONVERGENCE / OUTSIDE-VIEW CHECK

Before analysing any individual company, the book asks the "outside view" question: does this industry, as a whole, let its participants earn returns above the cost of capital?

Looking at the largest, most liquid names in this universe — Indian Hotels (Taj), ITC Hotels, EIH, Chalet Hotels, Leela Palaces, Ventive Hospitality, Lemon Tree — only one of seven clears 20% ROCE. The median ROCE of this large-cap cohort is roughly 15–16%, comfortably below Nalanda's bar, even at the top of a historically strong demand cycle.

This places Indian listed hospitality squarely in the book's third convergence bucket: "Only some companies make money — must be the undisputed leader to consider." It is not a uniformly attractive industry (bucket 2) and it does not structurally destroy value either (bucket 1) — it is a bifurcated industry where a small number of asset-light or irreplaceable-location operators earn excellent returns and the rest compete for occupancy on thinner margins. That context matters for everything that follows: the four names that survive Gate 1 below are, almost by definition, the exceptions rather than the rule — which is exactly the kind of business Nalanda is hunting for, but also exactly the kind of business that deserves more scrutiny than the headline ROCE number alone provides.

PART 5 — GATE 1: AVOID BIG RISKS (applied to the 6 screen-passers + 1 near-miss)

🔴 I T D C — DISQUALIFY (Filter 1.1)

The President of India holds 87.03% of ITDC as the controlling promoter; it is a Central Public Sector Undertaking under the Ministry of Tourism. The book is explicit and absolute on this: "We will not invest in a government-owned enterprise at any price." ROCE of 29.89% and recent strong quarterly profit growth are irrelevant — this is a hard stop at Filter 1.1. (As a secondary, independent reason to avoid: ITDC also trades at a P/E of ~64x with revenue that fell ~29% YoY in its most recent quarter — there is no valuation case here either.)

Verdict: DISQUALIFY — government-owned enterprise.

🔴 Asian Hotels (West) — DISQUALIFY (Filter 1.1)

Narrowly misses the ROCE screen (19.66%) but is flagged here because its conspicuously low P/E (9.0x) could tempt a value screen. The company was admitted into Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process under the IBC in September 2022; the promoter only regained control via a one-time-settlement approved by the NCLAT in January 2024. An NCLT/insolvency history is exactly the kind of fact Filter 1.1 instructs investors to disqualify on, regardless of how cheap the stock looks afterward.

Verdict: DISQUALIFY — NCLT/insolvency history.

🟡 U P Hotels — MONITOR, do not buy now (Filter 1.1 concern)

Owner of the Clarks hotel group (Agra, Jaipur, Lucknow, Khajuraho), debt-free, ROCE 21.87%. However, the company is currently the subject of a SEBI administrative warning/advisory letter (dated 21 May 2026), and its FY26 secretarial compliance report flags minimum-public-shareholding (MPS) fines and a promoter demat shortfall, alongside an active voluntary delisting process with an extension request. Free float and trading liquidity have historically been very thin (demat ratio as low as ~25% in past disclosures). The book's instruction is to disqualify on any regulatory censure; a live SEBI warning plus an unresolved delisting attempt is enough to keep this name out of "buy" territory until the situation is fully resolved and disclosed cleanly for at least one full reporting cycle.

Verdict: MONITOR — re-review after the SEBI matter and delisting process are resolved. Do not buy now.

🟢 EIH Ltd — PASS all six filters

  • 1.1 Integrity: No fraud/regulatory history found. Promoter group (Oberoi family, via Oberoi Hotels Pvt Ltd and related entities) holds ~32–36% and remains the largest single block. Governance nuance worth noting, not disqualifying: Reliance Industries holds ~18.8% (brought in historically as a "friendly" anchor investor to forestall an ITC stake-building campaign that has run since the early 2000s), and ITC itself still holds ~13–15%. This three-way structure has been stable for over a decade and has not produced any related-party abuse or governance breach — but it is a more complex ownership map than a simple founder-controlled company, and is worth watching for changes.
  • 1.2 Turnaround check: Not a turnaround story. EIH has been the flagship of the Oberoi Group since the late 1940s; current performance reflects a real demand cycle, not a hoped-for fix.
  • 1.3 Debt: Almost debt-free per latest disclosures; net debt position comfortably negative (net cash). Passes comfortably under the 0.3 D/E ceiling.
  • 1.4 Serial acquirer: No pattern of repeated M&A; growth has been organic/greenfield plus selective management contracts (including a recent co-management tie-up with Reliance on specific properties, not a value-destructive acquisition pattern).
  • 1.5 Pace of change: Luxury hospitality is a slow-moving, real-asset, brand- and location-driven industry — not a fast-changing one. Online disintermediation risk exists but has not eroded incumbents' structural position to date.
  • 1.6 Ownership/alignment: Promoter group is the single largest shareholder; not government-owned; not a diversified conglomerate (hospitality is EIH's only business).

Verdict: PASS — proceed to Gate 2.

🟢 EIH Associated Hotels — PASS all six filters

A joint venture between the Oberoi family and the Rajan Raheja Group (promoter holding ~75%), operating Oberoi/Trident-branded luxury properties. Almost debt-free, no acquisition history, no regulatory flags found, single-industry focus. Same luxury-hospitality industry characteristics as EIH (slow-changing, brand- and location-driven).

Verdict: PASS — proceed to Gate 2.

🟢 Benares Hotels — PASS all six filters, with one qualitative note

Operates Taj Ganges and Taj Nadesar Palace in Varanasi (plus a Ginger property in Gondia). Important correction to a common assumption: Benares Hotels is not part of the Oberoi/EIH group — it has been a subsidiary of The Indian Hotels Company Limited (IHCL/Tata Group) since 2011, operating under the Taj brand. Debt-free, consistent dividend payer, no fraud/regulatory history. The qualitative note for Filter 1.6: the largest shareholder is a corporate parent (IHCL) rather than an individual founder-entrepreneur with direct skin in the game — this is a different ownership character than Nalanda's stated preference for entrepreneur-promoter businesses, though it is not a misaligned or government owner either, so it does not trigger a disqualification, only a flag for the robustness assessment below.

Verdict: PASS (with ownership-character note) — proceed to Gate 2.

🟡 Sayaji Hotels (Pune) — PASS Gate 1, flagged for Gate 2/3 caution

A single-hotel entity demerged from parent Sayaji Hotels Ltd and separately listed in January 2024. Promoter holding ~75%, no fraud or litigation history found, not government-owned, not a serial acquirer, hospitality is a slow-changing industry. Two practical caveats carried forward: (a) as an independently listed and reported entity it has under 2.5 years of standalone track record, well short of the 5–10 year history the book wants before trusting a ROCE figure; (b) it is a single-asset, single-city business with very thin trading liquidity.

Verdict: PASS Gate 1 (conditionally) — proceed to Gate 2 with caution on track record and liquidity.

PART 6 — GATE 2: QUALITY AT A FAIR PRICE (the 4 names that reach this stage)

Current Sensex/Nifty TTM PE for reference: ~20.1–20.6x (June 2026). Nalanda's target entry zone = 25–30% discount to this = ~14.1–15.1x TTM PE.

EIH Ltd

  • 2.1 ROCE: 20.69% current, just above the 20% line. Company-level data shows a strong post-COVID recovery (PAT swung from a loss in FY2021 to ~₹711 Cr in FY2025), but this is a recovery trajectory, not yet a proven 10-year-average >20% track record — readers should pull the full pre-COVID decade before treating this as confirmed.
  • 2.2 Robustness: Balance sheet — strong, near-debt-free, consistent positive FCF (₹1,633 Cr over 5 years, accelerating). Competitive advantage — concrete and nameable: the Oberoi and Trident brands occupy genuinely scarce, hard-to-replicate locations (e.g., heritage and flagship urban/leisure sites) with decades of service-quality reputation; this is a real moat, not a marketing claim. Customer fragmentation — naturally excellent; hospitality is a B2C, walk-in/OTA-driven business with no concentration risk of the kind the book warns about (e.g., the WNS example). Management stability — Oberoi family management has run the company for three generations. Industry attractiveness — per Part 4, EIH is one of the few names that clears the bar in a bifurcated industry, which is itself supportive of a durable advantage rather than a cyclical fluke — but recent results show some margin softness (Q4 EBITDA margin fell to ~43% from ~47% a year earlier) worth watching. Verdict: HIGH ROBUSTNESS, with a watch-item on near-term margin trend.
  • 2.3 Management quality: Conservative balance sheet management through the COVID shock, consistent dividend payer, no value-destructive M&A on record. Verdict: GOOD (not yet rated EXCELLENT pending a longer post-recovery track record).
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~28.9x vs Sensex ~20.3x — EIH trades at roughly a 40% premium to the index, the opposite of the 25–30% discount required. Verdict: FAIL on price — no margin of safety today.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: The recent margin softness and the Jan-2026 demand wobble (terror-related disruption, FDTL flight cancellations) both look like proximate, temporary effects against a backdrop of structurally strong sector demand — not evidence of fundamental deterioration. Verdict: PROXIMATE FEAR ONLY at the sector level — but the stock is not cheap, so this isn't a "buy the dip" signal yet.

Gate 2 Summary: Quality looks genuinely high; price is not right. → WATCHLIST.

EIH Associated Hotels

  • 2.1 ROCE: 21.14%, modestly above threshold; screener-level data shows ROCE has been rising over the last three years, a healthier signal than a one-off spike.
  • 2.2 Robustness: Almost debt-free, healthy ~23% dividend payout, debtor days improving — balance sheet robust. Same Oberoi/Trident brand moat as EIH (it operates under the same brands). Customer base fragmented by nature of the industry. Management (Oberoi/Raheja JV structure) has been stable for decades. Verdict: HIGH ROBUSTNESS.
  • 2.3 Management quality: Consistent dividends, improving working-capital metrics, no M&A pattern. Verdict: GOOD.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~22.1x vs Sensex ~20.3x — roughly at or modestly above the index, not at a discount. Verdict: at/above index PE → WATCHLIST per the book's own valuation rule, not a FAIL, but not a buy signal either.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: No company-specific red flags found; performance tracks the same structural sector tailwind as EIH.

Gate 2 Summary: Quality high; price close to fair but not at the required discount. → WATCHLIST (closest of the four to a buyable price).

Benares Hotels

  • 2.1 ROCE: 29.83%, the highest of the surviving names, with reported 3-year ROE around 26–27% — a genuinely strong number, but earned from a single, small property base (~230 rooms at Taj Ganges plus Taj Nadesar Palace and one Ginger property), so the sample of "what could go wrong" is narrower than a multi-property operator.
  • 2.2 Robustness: Virtually debt-free balance sheet, steady FCF generation (smaller absolute scale: ~₹85 Cr over 5 years). Competitive advantage is real but geographically concentrated — the Taj brand plus an irreplaceable location near Varanasi's temple district, now benefiting from the government-backed Kashi Vishwanath Corridor tourism push. Customer/supplier fragmentation — fine, standard for the industry. Concentration risk is the open question here: single-city, effectively single-asset-class exposure to Varanasi tourism flows. A new 100-room tower (commissioned in FY25 at ~80% opening occupancy) is a sensible capacity response to demand, but it is incremental capacity in one city, not diversification. Verdict: MODERATE-TO-HIGH ROBUSTNESS — excellent unit economics, but single-city concentration is a real structural limitation the book's robustness test is designed to flag.
  • 2.3 Management quality: Consistent dividend payer (₹25/share recommended for FY26), unmodified audit opinions, sensible capacity expansion timed to a real demand driver. Verdict: GOOD.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~32.1x vs Sensex ~20.3x — roughly a 58% premium to the index. Verdict: FAIL on price — expensive, no margin of safety.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: No fundamental deterioration found; the stock's premium valuation reflects genuine structural tourism tailwinds (Kashi corridor) rather than a fear-driven dislocation — i.e., there is no "fear gap" to exploit here at all; it is simply priced for its quality. Verdict: NO GAP — EXPENSIVE.

Gate 2 Summary: Highest quality numbers of the four, but also the most expensive and the most geographically concentrated. → WATCHLIST, wait for a meaningful correction; size any eventual position with the concentration risk in mind.

Sayaji Hotels (Pune)

  • 2.1 ROCE: 28.40% — strong on paper, but with under 2.5 years of standalone listed history, this cannot yet be verified as a 5–10 year median in the way the framework requires.
  • 2.2 Robustness: This is where the cracks show. Reported free cash flow is very weak relative to reported profit — just ₹1.76 Cr in the most recent year and ₹17.05 Cr cumulative over three years, against annual profit running at roughly ₹20–24 Cr a quarter on a much smaller revenue base (~₹22 Cr/quarter). A ROCE/PAT figure that strong but cash generation that thin is a genuine yellow flag worth investigating — typically the gap is capex (the company is investing) or working-capital movement, but the book's robustness test explicitly wants "FCF consistently positive over 5+ years," and there simply isn't enough history to confirm that yet. Customer fragmentation is fine (industry-standard). Competitive advantage — a single Pune property; advantage is plausible (mid-market positioning, established Sayaji brand) but not demonstrated at scale or over time as an independent entity. Trading liquidity is extremely thin (reported daily volumes have at times been in single digits), which matters in practice for anyone trying to build or exit a meaningful "permanent" position. Verdict: LOW-TO-MODERATE ROBUSTNESS — promising numbers, insufficient independent track record, and a real cash-conversion question mark.
  • 2.3 Management quality: Too early to judge from historical actions as an independent entity; parent group (Sayaji Hotels Ltd) has a much longer history but with weaker consolidated numbers (parent entity ROE is currently negative, per the screen above) — worth understanding why the Pune subsidiary and the parent diverge so sharply before drawing conclusions about capital allocation discipline group-wide. Verdict: MEDIOCRE-TO-UNPROVEN, pending more history.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~12.35x vs Sensex ~20.3x — this does clear the 25–30% discount bar on paper (roughly a 39% discount). However, such a low multiple on a thinly-traded micro-cap is at least as likely to reflect an illiquidity/uncertainty discount as genuine undervaluation of a quality business — the valuation signal here is less reliable than for a liquid, well-covered stock. Verdict: technically PASSES the PE/index-discount test, but the quality case is not yet proven enough to treat this as a high-conviction "price is right" situation.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: Not enough independent history to separate proximate noise from ultimate quality.

Gate 2 Summary: Cheapest of the four on paper, but the thinnest evidence base and the only one with an internal cash-flow red flag. → MONITOR rather than WATCHLIST — re-examine after 2–3 more annual reporting cycles as a standalone entity, and specifically reconcile the profit-vs-FCF gap before considering this further.

PART 10 — CONTEXT: WHY THE WELL-KNOWN NAMES DIDN'T MAKE IT

Several companies in this list are widely covered, well-regarded, and popular with growth-oriented investors, but fail Nalanda's specific ROCE bar today. Understanding why is itself a useful application of the framework's "common mistakes" checklist:

  • Indian Hotels Co. (Taj/IHCL) — the largest and best-known name (₹1.04 lakh Cr market cap), trading at ~60x TTM PE with ROCE of 17.06%. IHCL is executing a genuinely sound asset-light expansion strategy and analyst consensus is constructive (Nomura initiated coverage with a Buy rating and ~₹823–830 target, citing strong ADR growth visibility), but the framework explicitly rejects investing on the basis of future ROCE improvement: "We don't listen to stories about how ROCE will improve in the future." IHCL is a MONITOR-worthy quality franchise, not a current Darwin-qualified buy.
  • ITC Hotels — ROCE 10.73%, recently demerged from ITC Ltd; too early to have an independent ROCE track record at all, and current returns are well below threshold.
  • Chalet Hotels & Ventive Hospitality — both real-estate-heavy, asset-owning growth stories (ROCE 17.07% and 10.81% respectively) still in a capex/ramp-up phase; classic examples of growth narratives that have not yet converted into the ROCE the framework demands.
  • Lemon Tree Hotels — a well-run, asset-light-transitioning mid-market operator with management explicitly targeting >20% ROCE by FY26, debt/equity improving to ~0.86x. This is precisely the kind of "story about how ROCE will improve" the book tells investors to ignore until the number actually arrives — Lemon Tree is worth re-screening once (and if) it clears 20% ROCE on a sustained basis, not before.
  • Mahindra Holiday & Resorts, Juniper Hotels, Samhi Hotels — ROCE of 7.75%, 8.05%, and 8.92% respectively; all well below threshold despite reasonable brand recognition and, in Samhi's case, a notably low headline P/E (9.65x) that does not compensate for a sub-threshold ROCE.

PART 11 — RISKS & MONITORING TRIGGERS FOR THE WATCHLIST NAMES

  • EIH / EIH Associated Hotels: Watch for (a) a market correction that brings TTM PE toward the 14–15x zone, (b) confirmation that the FY2025–26 margin softness is cyclical rather than competitive/structural, (c) any change in the Oberoi–RIL–ITC shareholding equilibrium.
  • Benares Hotels: Watch for (a) valuation compression toward fair value, (b) occupancy ramp-up of the new 100-room tower, (c) any policy or infrastructure change affecting Varanasi tourism flow (upside or downside).
  • U P Hotels: Watch for formal resolution of the SEBI administrative warning and the voluntary delisting process; re-screen only after clean disclosures resume.
  • Sayaji Hotels (Pune): Watch for (a) at least 2 more years of independently reported FCF data, (b) an explanation for the wide gap between reported profit and reported free cash flow, (c) any improvement in trading liquidity.
