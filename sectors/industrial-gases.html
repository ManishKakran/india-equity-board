PART A — INDUSTRY ANALYSIS

A.1 What this industry actually is

Industrial gases (oxygen, nitrogen, argon, hydrogen, CO₂, helium, acetylene, specialty/electronic gases) are produced mainly by cryogenic air separation and sold through three channels: - On-site / pipeline — a dedicated plant built next to one large customer (steel, refining), gas piped directly, contracts run 15–20 years. - Merchant / bulk liquid — gas trucked in cryogenic tankers to mid-size industrial and hospital customers. - Packaged / cylinders — gas in cylinders for small workshops, welding, and healthcare.

This is a capital-intensive, regulated, infrastructure-like business: an Air Separation Unit (ASU) costs tens to hundreds of crores, takes 18–24 months to build, and is economically viable only with a long-term contracted customer (often steel or chemicals). That is the source of whatever moat exists in this industry — not patents, not brands.

A.2 Market size and growth

  • Global industrial gases market: roughly USD 118 billion (2025), growing at a moderate ~4–5% CAGR; the top five players — Air Liquide, Linde plc, Air Products, Messer, and Nippon Sanso — control ~80% of the global market, making this one of the most consolidated industrial sectors anywhere.
  • India industrial gases market: independent estimates range from USD 1.4–4.9 billion depending on scope (pure gas sales vs. gas + engineering + cylinders), growing at ~5.5–7.5% CAGR through 2030–32. The wide range across research houses reflects how fragmented reporting is below the top few players — itself a sign this is a niche, thinly-covered sector for equity research.
  • India's top 5 players hold ~50–80% share depending on the estimate, but almost none of them are listed: INOX Air Products (largest in India by capacity — part of the INOX/Gujarat Fluorochemicals group, unlisted), Air Liquide India, Messer India, and Air Water India are all unlisted MNC subsidiaries or JVs. Linde India and Ellenbarrie are effectively the only two listed companies with real scale; everything else in the Indian listed space is a sub-₹100 crore regional or family-run operator.

A.3 What is actually driving demand right now (2025–26)

  • Steel capacity expansion is the single largest driver — every new steel furnace needs oxygen and argon, and India's steel capacity additions (SAIL, Tata Steel, JSW) are driving a wave of new ASU contracts. Linde signed a new ASU for SAIL's Rourkela plant; INOX Air Products commissioned a 2,150 TPD unit at SAIL Bokaro and is adding another large unit there.
  • Electronics/semiconductors and clean hydrogen are the new long-duration growth vectors globally (Linde plc commissioned a new high-purity nitrogen/oxygen ASU in China for electronics in Feb 2026; INOX Air Products launched a green hydrogen facility for Asahi India Glass in March 2025).
  • Healthcare/medical oxygen remains a steady base-load demand, with memory of COVID-era shortages still shaping capacity planning.
  • Argon and specialty gases for welding, defence, and railways are the higher-margin pockets that smaller, newer entrants (Ellenbarrie's Uluberia-II expansion, Stallion's R-32 plant) are chasing because commodity oxygen/nitrogen margins are thinner.

A.4 The Outside View / Convergence Test (per the framework: "if most companies in an industry can't hack it, why believe this one is unique?")

Applying the book's three-way convergence test to industrial gases in India:

  1. Structural barrier to entry is real and durable. ASUs are capital-intensive, contracts are 15–20 years long, and customers (a steel plant) cannot easily switch suppliers mid-contract. This is the kind of slow-changing, rule-stable industry the framework favours (Gate 1 Filter 1.5 passes easily for the industry as a whole).
  2. But realised ROCE across the industry is structurally below the framework's 20% bar. Even the global, debt-free, market-leading entity in this list — Linde India — has not sustainably cleared 20% ROCE in over a decade (detail in Part B). High capex intensity, long payback ASUs, and continuous reinvestment keep capital employed high relative to EBIT even when the business itself is safe and entrenched. The reason is structural, not company-specific: a 20-year pipeline gas contract is "safe," but it ties up capital for 20 years.
  3. Convergence conclusion (using the book's own three categories): "Only some companies make money — must be the undisputed leader to consider." Within this list, capacity/scale leadership (Linde, and to a lesser extent Ellenbarrie) does correlate with somewhat better and more stable returns than the sub-scale family operators. But "somewhat better" is still below, not above, Nalanda's 20% minimum. The honest convergence verdict is: this industry is safe but not the kind of high-ROCE compounder the Siberian Fox test is designed to find — at least not among the names currently listed in India.

This single industry-level finding is the most important conclusion of this report and explains why almost every company below fails Gate 2's primary filter even where Gate 1 (safety) is otherwise clean.

A.5 Industry-wide capex super-cycle and why free cash flow is negative across the board

Every company of any real size in this list — Linde, Ellenbarrie, Stallion — shows negative cumulative free cash flow over the last 3–5 years, even though each is profitable and (mostly) growing earnings. This is not an operational red flag in isolation; it reflects a sector-wide capacity build-out cycle (new ASUs for steel, new argon/specialty lines, new R-32/refrigerant capacity) funded from internal accruals, IPO proceeds, or modest debt. The framework's balance-sheet robustness test ("is FCF consistently positive over 5+ years?") is genuinely failed by this sector right now — but the reason matters for judgement, even though it does not change the strict verdict.

Gate 2 — shown for completeness only (not required once Gate 1 fails, but instructive)

2.1 Historical ROCE — the primary filter. Linde India's own 10-year ROCE trail (standalone, screener-sourced): 3% (2014) → 3% → 4% → 5% → 6% → 11% → 10% → 17% (2021) → 21% (FY23, 15-month transition year) → 17% (FY24) → 17% (FY25) → 18% (FY26). The median over this entire decade is roughly 11–12%, with exactly one year crossing 20%. This fails the framework's stated threshold ("reject companies with long-term historical ROCE lower than 20 percent... over the past five to ten years or more") just as clearly as the governance issue does. Even the highest-quality, most dominant, debt-free company in this entire universe has never been a sustained >20% ROCE compounder — it is a safe, well-run, but capital-heavy infrastructure business, not a Siberian-Fox-style high-ROCE compounder.

2.4 Valuation. Current Stock P/E is ~105–110x trailing earnings against a current Sensex TTM PE of ~20x — a premium of roughly 5x the index, the opposite of the 25–30% discount the framework requires. (This valuation is driven by the stock's status as the only large, debt-free, MNC-backed pure-play listed gases franchise in India — a scarcity premium, not a Nalanda-style bargain.)

FINAL VERDICT: ❌ AVOID — Gate 1 Filter 1.1 fails (regulatory censure / related-party transaction dispute, unresolved); independently, Gate 2's ROCE filter also fails (10-year median well below 20%); independently again, valuation is at an extreme premium, not a discount. Three separate, independent reasons to avoid, none requiring any interpretive stretch of the framework.

What would change this: a Supreme Court resolution that clears Linde India's RPT governance overhang, combined with several more years of ROCE sustainably above 20% (currently trending the right direction at 17–18%, helped by margin improvement from 24% to ~31–36% OPM), would be the trigger to revisit Gate 1 and Gate 2 from scratch. Until then this is not a permanent-ownership candidate by the book's own rules — even though it is, by a distance, the best-run business in this list.

Gate 2 — Buy High Quality at a Fair Price

2.1 Historical ROCE. Ellenbarrie's own ROCE history (screener-sourced, FY16–FY25): 1% → 2% → 1% → 5% → 6% → 8% → 53% (FY20, an outlier year driven by a one-off ₹121 Cr "other income" item, not core operations) → 15% (FY21) → 21% (FY22) → 8% (FY23) → 13% (FY24) → 18% (FY25, latest). Excluding the clear one-off year, the genuine trend is a long run well below 20% that has only recently, in the last 1–2 years, approached the threshold as capacity utilisation and margins improved (operating margin moved from ~16% in FY20 to ~35% in FY25, per the company's own investor disclosures). Verdict: FAIL (current/median ROCE is at, not durably above, 20%) — but this is a borderline, improving fail, not a structural one.

2.2 Robustness. - Balance sheet: Improving — net debt-free target achieved post-IPO; FCF has been negative in 4 of the last 5 years (-₹188 Cr cumulative over 5 years, -₹132 Cr over 3 years, -₹65 Cr last year) due to the Uluberia-II ASU build — explainable capex, not distress, but it does mean the balance-sheet-robustness test is not yet cleanly passed. - Competitive advantage: Largest 100%-Indian-owned gases company by installed capacity; over 50 years of customer relationships (AIIMS, Dr. Reddy's, Tata-group steel accounts, defence). Real, but regional (East and South India) rather than pan-India like Linde. - Customer concentration: Top 10 customers were ~41% of gas revenue in FY24 — inside the book's >25% "watch" zone but not at the extreme WNS-style 55% the book specifically flags as disqualifying. Worth monitoring, not a hard fail. - Government/PSU exposure: >20% of FY24 revenue came from government/PSU tenders — a concentration and pricing-power risk worth tracking, though this does not trigger the Filter 1.1 "government-owned enterprise" rule, since Ellenbarrie itself is privately promoter-owned. - Industry attractiveness: per Part A, structurally safe but not high-ROCE.

Verdict: MODERATE ROBUSTNESS — improving balance sheet and margins, real regional moat, but customer concentration and negative FCF keep this short of "high."

2.3 Management Quality. Capital allocation has been reasonable: used the IPO specifically to delever and fund a high-margin argon/specialty capacity expansion rather than diversify; no value-destructive M&A. Promoter sold a moderate stake via OFS at IPO (standard practice, not a governance flag) and stake has been stable at 77% since. Verdict: GOOD.

2.4 Valuation. Current TTM PE is ~28–38x (depending on date) against a current Sensex TTM PE of ~20x — a premium of roughly 40–90%, the opposite of the 25–30% discount required. Verdict: FAIL — no margin of safety.

2.5 Proximate vs Ultimate Cause. Not applicable in the "stock is cheap because of noise" sense — Ellenbarrie is expensive because it is the only listed pure-Indian-owned scaled gases business and carries an IPO scarcity premium, not because the market is mispricing a temporary problem.

GATE 2 SUMMARY: NO — MONITOR AT LOWER PRICE. ROCE is borderline (improving, not yet durably >20%), robustness is moderate, and valuation offers no discount at all.

FINAL VERDICT: 📋 MONITOR — Gate 1 passes cleanly (this is the one company in the list with no governance, debt, or ownership red flags), but Gate 2 quality (ROCE durability) and valuation both fall short today. Per the framework's own MONITOR definition: review again after 2 annual report cycles — specifically, watch for (a) ROCE sustaining above 20% for 2+ consecutive years as the new argon capacity ramps, (b) FCF turning durably positive once the Uluberia-II capex cycle completes, and (c) the stock price (or earnings growth) closing the valuation gap to the Sensex.

PART E — THE HONEST INDUSTRY-LEVEL TAKEAWAY

This sector illustrates something the framework is explicit about but is easy to miss: a "safe," consolidated, high-entry-barrier industry is not automatically a high-ROCE industry. Industrial gases in India is genuinely slow-changing, oligopolistic, and protected by real capital and contractual barriers — exactly the kind of industry structure Nalanda's "outside view" test looks for. But the capital intensity of building and continuously expanding ASUs keeps capital employed high enough that even the best-run, debt-free, dominant player in the country (Linde India) has not produced a sustained >20% ROCE track record in over a decade. Layer onto that a live SEBI/SAT/Supreme Court related-party-transaction dispute at the one company that might otherwise have passed Gate 1, a near-halving of promoter ownership at the second-largest pure play, and a long tail of sub-scale, weakly-governed micro-caps, and the conclusion is unambiguous: today's listed Indian industrial-gases universe does not contain a Nalanda-style permanent-ownership candidate. Ellenbarrie is the one name worth watching for a possible future re-rating in quality (not price), and even there, patience — not action — is the correct posture right now.
