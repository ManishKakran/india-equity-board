🧬 81. Housing Finance Companies — Darwin Investor Framework Analysis

Applying the Nalanda Capital / Pulak Prasad methodology — zero interpretive additions

Analysis date: June 2026 | Universe: 19 listed Indian Housing Finance Companies (HFCs)

INDUSTRY OVERVIEW — 2026

India's mortgage finance market has kept compounding through FY26 even as the mix shifts. ICRA's April 2026 sector note puts HFCs' on-book portfolio above ₹10 lakh crore, with performance expected to stay broadly stable. Within that, the affordable housing finance (A-HFC) sub-segment — loans up to ₹35 lakh — is the real growth engine: CRISIL Ratings projects A-HFC AUM growth of 20–21% in both FY26 and FY27, building on ~23% growth in FY25, even as it flags a moderation from the loan-against-property (LAP) boom of the last two years. CARE Ratings' March 2026 research sizes total A-HFC AUM at ~₹1.55 lakh crore as of March 2025, and ICRA's July 2025 sector report projects this reaching ₹2.5 lakh crore by FY28.

The growth has a clear structural driver: prime/large HFCs have been pushed upmarket by bank competition on ticket sizes above ₹35 lakh, vacating the ₹15–35 lakh bracket that specialised affordable-housing financiers (Aptus, Aadhar, Home First, India Shelter, AAVAS) have moved into aggressively.

The one genuine yellow flag in an otherwise constructive growth story: CRISIL data shows more than 70% of A-HFCs reported a rise in 90+ day overdue loans in the sub-₹15 lakh bracket between FY24 and FY25, a trend that continued into H1 FY26 — attributed to borrower over-leverage, seasoning, and spillover stress from adjacent microfinance segments. Credit costs are expected to edge up gradually as a result, even as overall profitability stays healthy (CRISIL projects ~2.5% return on managed assets across both fiscals).

Regulatory architecture: HFCs are now regulated by the RBI under its harmonised Scale-Based Regulation framework (NHB's supervisory role having been folded into RBI in 2021). The regulatory leverage ceiling — borrowings-to-net-owned-funds — sits at roughly 12–13x. As a 2019 NHB directive on balance-sheet strengthening shows, even India's largest, most conservatively run HFCs (HDFC, the erstwhile Indiabulls Housing, PNB Housing, LIC Housing) were running leverage of 8–11x at the time and had to raise equity simply to stay under the ceiling. This single fact turns out to matter more than anything else in this report.

STEP ZERO — THE OUTSIDE VIEW (CONVERGENCE TEST), APPLIED AT THE INDUSTRY LEVEL

The framework is explicit that the industry base rate must be assessed before any individual company is analysed:

"If the industry allows its companies to make money consistently, we love it; if not, we better have a perfect reason for spending even one minute analysing a business."

Housing finance passes the "makes money consistently" test in the ordinary sense — these are profitable, growing, well-capitalised businesses by financial-sector standards. But Nalanda's own quantitative gates are not bank-sector gates; they are built for operating businesses, and applying them literally to a regulated lending industry produces a structural verdict that has nothing to do with any individual company's competence.

The leverage conflict (Gate 1, Filter 1.3) is not a company-specific finding — it is definitional

The book's tolerance is explicit and unambiguous: maximum D/E of 0.3, ideally zero debt, interest coverage comfortably above 10x. A Housing Finance Company's entire reason for existing is to borrow money cheaply (via NCDs, bank lines, NHB refinance, deposits) and lend it out at a regulated spread. There is no version of this business that runs at D/E ≤ 0.3 — doing so would mean abandoning the lending model altogether. The NHB 2019 reference point above (8–11x leverage at India's most conservative large HFCs, against a regulatory ceiling of ~12–13x) is not an outlier data point; it is the floor of how this industry is structured.

This means every one of the 19 companies in this list fails Gate 1, Filter 1.3, before any company-specific work begins — not because of poor capital allocation, but because of what the business is. Per the framework's own rule — "Fail any filter in Gate 1 → stop immediately. Do not proceed" — that is, strictly speaking, the end of the analysis for all 19 names.

Why the ROCE numbers in the source data look so low — and why that's not really an ROCE problem

The book's ROCE formula is EBIT ÷ (Net Working Capital + Net Fixed Assets) — designed to measure how efficiently a business turns operating capital into profit, deliberately excluding the financing structure. For a lending business, almost the entire balance sheet is the financing structure: the "capital employed" denominator is dominated by the loan book, which is itself funded mostly by borrowed money, not equity. Diluting returns by a leverage-funded asset base is mechanically guaranteed to produce a depressed ROCE figure, regardless of underwriting quality. That is exactly the pattern in the source data: every name in this list shows ROCE in the high single digits to mid-teens (vs. the 20% minimum), while reporting respectable ROE of 12–20% — the gap between the two is the leverage itself, not a quality signal.

Put plainly: ROCE is the wrong lens for a lending business, and the book offers no alternative formula for financials. That omission is itself informative — it is consistent with Nalanda's portfolio (~30 businesses, overwhelmingly debt-free operating companies) simply not containing banks or NBFC lenders. The framework, read literally and without embellishment, structurally excludes the entire BFSI lending sector — this report is the first in the series where that is the headline finding rather than a valuation call.

Convergence conclusion

Per the book's three stated outcomes — (1) industry structurally destroys value, (2) industry is uniformly attractive, (3) only some companies make money, must find the leader — none of the three actually fits a leveraged-lending industry cleanly, because the convergence test itself assumes an operating-business framework. The honest conclusion: this industry sits outside the framework's domain of applicability. It is not "avoid because it's bad"; it is "avoid because the tool doesn't measure this kind of business correctly, and the book's own hard ceiling (D/E ≤ 0.3) is incompatible with how the business model works by definition."

Two patterns worth noting honestly:

  • Several of the cheapest stocks on trailing P/E in this list are the lowest-quality by ROCE/ROE. LIC Housing Finance (P/E 5.4x), Repco Home Finance (P/E 5.1x) and GIC Housing Finance (P/E 5.8x) all trade well below the current Sensex TTM PE of ~20.1x (as of June 2026), and well inside Nalanda's stated 25–30% discount band — but their ROCE/ROE profile is at the weaker end of the peer set, not the stronger end. A low multiple here is not "Nalanda-style margin of safety on a great business"; it looks more consistent with the market correctly discounting government-linked-promoter inefficiency and structurally lower growth. This is a useful reminder that cheapness alone, divorced from the quality gate, is a value trap rather than a signal.
  • Aptus Value Housing Finance is the standout on every quality dimension simultaneously — highest ROCE (15.4%) and ROE (20.1%) in the peer set, lowest leverage by a wide margin (D/E ≈1.6x against a sector norm of 3–11x), and a genuine founder-promoter (M. Anandan) rather than a financial sponsor. Its current P/E of 14.06x is, by coincidence, inside Nalanda's documented 14.9x median entry multiple. None of this changes the Gate 1 verdict — Filter 1.3 still disqualifies it on leverage, and its ROCE still falls short of the 20% minimum — but if this sector were ever revisited with a financial-services-appropriate methodology, this is the one name worth tracking informally outside the strict framework.

Since Gate 1 already disqualifies every name in this list, valuation is not a live decision variable here — but for completeness, only three names in the set currently trade inside or near that entry band: Repco Home Finance (5.1x), GIC Housing Finance (5.8x), and LIC Housing Finance (5.4x) — all three of which, as noted above, sit on the weaker end of the quality spectrum and carry the government-promoter flag. Bajaj Housing Finance (28.5x) and Piramal Finance (203.7x — earnings near-zero on a stressed-asset legacy book) sit at the opposite extreme.

No company in this list clears Gate 1. This is the first sector report in this series where the disqualification is categorical rather than valuation-driven. In the Heavy Electrical Equipment review (#74), the conclusion was "good businesses, wrong price — wait." Here the conclusion is structurally different: the business model itself — borrowing money to lend it at a regulated spread — is incompatible with the framework's hard debt ceiling, independent of price, management quality, or cycle timing. A market correction that brought every HFC in this list to single-digit P/E tomorrow would not change that verdict, because Filter 1.3 is not a valuation filter.

Layered on top of that structural finding, three names (Sammaan Capital, Reliance Home Finance, Sahara Housing Fina Corp) carry independent, well-documented promoter-integrity disqualifiers serious enough that they would fail Gate 1 even in an unleveraged business. Five names trace promoter control back to the Government of India through a bank or insurer parent. Four are owned outright by financial sponsors rather than entrepreneurs. Only Bajaj Housing Finance and Aptus Value Housing Finance present a clean, founder/entrepreneur-anchored ownership structure — and Aptus, in particular, is worth keeping on an informal radar as the best-capitalised, highest-ROCE name in the sector, even though it does not clear this framework's gates either.

Why not state a one-line BUY/WATCHLIST thesis? The framework requires one only for a BUY or WATCHLIST decision. None applies here — the honest one-line summary is the inverse: "No Housing Finance Company qualifies as a permanent-ownership candidate under this framework, because the lending business model requires leverage far in excess of the book's 0.3 ceiling by structural necessity, not by any individual company's choice — a conclusion valuation cannot override."
