INDUSTRY OVERVIEW — ALUMINIUM (INDIA)

What the Industry Is

India's aluminium ecosystem spans primary smelting (bauxite → alumina → aluminium), downstream processing (rolled products, extrusions, wire rods, foils, castings), and secondary/recycling. The sector is broadly split into:

  • Primary producers (integrated mine-to-metal): Hindalco, NALCO, Vedanta/BALCO
  • Secondary/downstream processors (buy ingots, convert to value-added products): Arfin India, Maan Aluminium, MMP Industries, PG Foils, Jainik Power, etc.
  • Traders + micro-processors: Sacheta Metals, Golkonda Aluminium, Bothra Metals

Latest Industry Updates (2025–2026)

Market Size & Growth: India's aluminium market was valued at ~USD 15.5 billion in 2024, expected to reach USD 25 billion by 2030 at a CAGR of ~7.8%. Primary aluminium production runs at 4.1–4.2 million tonnes/year (FY2024), while domestic consumption is only 2.3–2.4 million tonnes — making India a significant net exporter.

Demand Drivers: - National Infrastructure Pipeline (₹111 lakh crore), EV adoption, renewable energy installations (solar mounting structures), and defence are structurally driving multi-decade demand. - Transportation sector aluminium demand growing at ~7.2% p.a.; electrical sector is the largest consumer.

LME Pricing: Goldman Sachs (April 2025) projected aluminium prices at ~$2,300/tonne by Q4 2026 from ~$2,700 now, with recovery to ~$2,800 by 2027 as supply deficits re-emerge. Near-term prices are range-bound at $2,000–$2,400/tonne due to China's overcapacity.

Policy Tailwinds: - Quality Control Order (QCO) effective October 1, 2025 — mandatory BIS certification for aluminium sheets, coils, rods, extrusions. This raises the bar for imports and consolidates market share toward certified domestic manufacturers. - Anti-dumping duty imposed on Chinese aluminium foil imports (up to 80 micron). Protects foil manufacturers like PG Foils. - India–UK FTA eliminates 2–10% duties on aluminium products — export opportunity for Indian producers. - INR 1,500 crore government incentive scheme for critical mineral recycling (FY2026–FY2031).

Carbon / Green Aluminium Risk: India's coal-heavy smelters face EU CBAM exposure from 2026 — benchmark emission intensity of ~1.55 tonnes CO₂ per tonne of aluminium. Many Indian smelters exceed this threshold, which is a long-term structural risk for exports to Europe.

Major Capex in Sector: - Hindalco announced INR 15,000 crore investment in Madhya Pradesh (Feb 2025) — expanding smelting capacity over two years. - Vedanta commissioned 1.5 MTPA expansion at Lanjigarh alumina refinery (2024), targeting 3 MTPA aluminium production. - Rio Tinto signed MoU with AMG Metals to explore a low-carbon, 1 MTPA aluminium project in India.

Industry Verdict: The sector is bifurcated. The top tier (NALCO, Jainik Power, Msafe, Sudal) earns ROCE well above the 12–15% threshold that signals a structurally attractive industry. The bulk of the peer group earns poor returns, reflecting the commodity/low-value-add nature of simple aluminium processing. This is NOT a structurally wide-moat industry — returns are highly company-specific, not industry-wide.

Structural Risks: 1. Commodity price pass-through: most processors are margin takers, not price setters 2. Chinese overcapacity creating persistent global supply surplus 3. Power cost sensitivity: aluminium smelting is energy-intensive; coal/renewables mix is a cost lever 4. Working capital intensity: metal trading businesses bloat capital employed

KEY DARWIN INSIGHTS FOR THIS SECTOR

  1. This is NOT a wide-moat industry. The median ROCE across all 17 companies is ~15–18% — barely above the cost of capital. Only NALCO, Jainik Power, Msafe, and Sudal earn genuinely superior returns. Selection within the sector, not sector allocation, is the key.

  2. The commodity trap: Most downstream processors cannot price above raw material input costs (aluminium ingots follow LME). Structural ROCE above 20% is only achievable via integration (NALCO), niche products (Msafe, Sudal), or unique positioning.

  3. Negative FCF is a red flag even at high ROCE. Arfin, Maan, Msafe, Manaksia, PG Foils, Kanishk all show negative FCF. The Nalanda framework demands that ROCE ultimately converts to free cash. Where it does not, ROCE may be illusory (aggressive working capital, capitalised expenses).

  4. Valuation discipline is critical here. Several companies (Arfin at 113x, Maan at 55x, Maitri at 156x, Bothra at 135x) trade at prohibitive multiples for businesses with sub-par returns. This is purely speculative — avoid entirely.

  5. The QCO and anti-dumping duties are proximate catalysts. They benefit the sector temporarily. The Darwin framework cautions against buying businesses solely on regulatory protection — this is a proximate cause, not an ultimate competitive advantage. A business must sustain its ROCE after the protection fades.
