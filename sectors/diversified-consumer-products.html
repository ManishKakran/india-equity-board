Industry Outlook (Latest Data — 2025–2026)

EMS / White Goods (VOEPL): India's EMS market was valued at ~USD 65 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow at a CAGR of 17.5% through 2032, reaching ~USD 198 billion. A higher-end projection from another source pegs this at a 30% CAGR through 2030, driven by the PLI (Production Linked Incentive) scheme, China+1 manufacturing strategy, and rising domestic consumer electronics demand. Government-approved PLI packages worth INR 22,919 crore for electronic components were announced in March 2025. India's share of the global EMS market is projected to leap from ~4% in 2024 to potentially one-third of incremental global EMS growth by 2030. However, this is a highly competitive, capital-intensive, margin-thin industry. ROCE across Indian EMS peers (Dixon, Amber, VOEPL) is highly variable, with many players earning well below 20% ROCE, which is the Nalanda minimum threshold.

Optical / Spectacle Lenses (Yash Optics): The India spectacles market was valued at USD 4.1 billion in 2024, growing at a projected CAGR of 11.4% through 2030. The optical lens segment is being driven by rising myopia (an estimated 10 million new cases of myopia emerge annually in India), digital screen usage, an ageing population, and government vision health initiatives. The market is fragmented at the domestic manufacturing end but dominated internationally by Essilor, Zeiss, Hoya, and Johnson & Johnson Vision. This creates a structural challenge for small Indian manufacturers competing on quality and distribution against global brands.

Lifestyle Consumer Products / Brand Licensing (Swiss Military): Highly fragmented, fashion-driven, low-barrier-to-entry sector. Brand licensing models in consumer goods require sustained marketing spend to maintain brand equity. The India luggage and lifestyle accessories market is growing but intensely competitive with both international brands (Samsonite, VIP) and domestic e-commerce players (Lenskart in eyewear analogy). The RFID seal business is a nascent diversification. ROCEs in this industry cluster are typically low unless a strong, defensible brand moat exists.

Convergence Conclusion (per Darwin Framework)

"If most companies in the industry can't hack it, why should I believe that this company is unique?"

The EMS and commodity consumer goods industries in India have structurally low and volatile ROCEs at the median participant level. This is not an industry where most companies earn returns above the cost of capital consistently. The optical lens industry is marginally better but still dominated by global incumbents. Any company in these industries must be the undisputed leader to even warrant analysis under Nalanda's framework.

GATE 1 — AVOID BIG RISKS

Filter 1.1 — Promoter / Management Integrity

Evaluation: - Present promoters took over in September 2020 (formerly Network Limited, a trading and investment company). The business has been transformed — it was previously engaged in capital market trading and F&O segments, a fundamentally different business. - The company has issued three rights issues since November 2021. Verdict: PASS with significant reservations — No formal integrity violation found, but repeated rights issues, short operating history under current promoters, and related-party loan conversions are yellow flags that make this business unsuitable for the "permanent ownership" intent of the framework. Per the book's principle: "Nalanda lost $50M at Warburg Pincus by ignoring informal buzz — never again." The informal signals here are cautionary.

KEY DARWIN FRAMEWORK LESSONS FROM THIS SCREENER

  1. ROCE is the single most important filter. All three companies fail the >20% ROCE test. This screener, classified as "Diversified Consumer Products," reflects an industry segment where most participants cannot consistently earn returns above the cost of capital — precisely what the convergence/outside view test predicts.

  2. Negative FCF is the natural consequence of low ROCE + high capex. All three companies have been persistent FCF burners over 5 years. This is not a "temporary" phenomenon but a structural feature of capital-intensive, low-ROCE businesses.

  3. The EMS/white-goods sector is a fast-scaling but low-ROCE industry. High revenue growth (VOEPL's 57% CAGR) does not translate to high ROCE in capital-intensive EMS. Nalanda explicitly avoids businesses where growth destroys rather than creates value.

  4. A clean promoter and slow-changing industry are necessary but not sufficient. Yash Optics passes Gate 1 cleanly — promoter integrity, no debt, slow-changing industry, no M&A. But without >20% ROCE, it cannot qualify for investment under this framework.

  5. Valuation across this screener is expensive. The lowest P/E in this basket is 36x (Yash Optics), against a Nalanda target entry of 14.9x. Even MONITORING companies at these prices represents a substantial wait for a meaningful correction.
