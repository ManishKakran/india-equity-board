PART 1: INDUSTRY OVERVIEW — THE OUTSIDE VIEW (Convergence Test)

"Before picking individual businesses, we look at the industry through an 'outside view' lens — using base rates of industry ROCE to understand whether the sector structurally allows participants to earn returns above the cost of capital." — Pulak Prasad

1.1 Sector Characteristics

What this sector does: These are predominantly downstream processors and recyclers — converting primary aluminium, copper, and zinc (produced by Hindalco, Vedanta, Hindustan Zinc) into value-added products: wires, rods, extrusions, ingots, billets, sheets, tubes, and specialty alloys. Most are secondary/recycling-based businesses using scrap metal as raw material, which structurally compresses margins but reduces exposure to primary commodity price swings.

Industry structure observations: - Highly fragmented: 25 listed companies, most with market caps under ₹500 crore. The top player (Precision Wires) at ₹6,874 crore is an outlier; the median market cap of this universe is ~₹175 crore. - Capital-light on assets, capital-intensive on working capital: These businesses carry large raw-material and receivable buffers, which is why free cash flow is negative for 17 of 25 companies. - Thin margin, high-volume processing: EBITDA margins in wire/rod/extrusion businesses typically range 3–8%. Recycling businesses can earn more if they have captive scrap sourcing or specialised alloy capability. - Commodity passthrough risk: Most businesses price products as "LME metal price + conversion/processing premium." The conversion margin — not the metal itself — is the true economic moat. Companies that cannot defend this margin get squeezed when input and output both track LME.

1.2 Structural Demand Drivers (Latest Data, 2025–2026)

The sector enjoys genuine structural tailwinds, though these apply unevenly across the value chain:

Aluminium: India's aluminium market was valued at ~USD 15.5 billion in 2024, expected to reach USD 25 billion by 2030 at a ~7.8% CAGR. India is the world's second-largest primary aluminium producer but per capita consumption of just 3.9 kg compares to a global average of 11 kg and China's 31.7 kg — implying significant domestic headroom. Key growth vectors: EV battery housings, solar panel frames, lightweight automotive components, power transmission cables (substituting copper), and infrastructure (metro, airports, smart cities).

Copper: India's copper demand is expected to grow ~7% annually, led by renewable energy, EVs, and industrial corridors. Domestic consumption already exceeds 750,000 tonnes against production of ~555,000 tonnes — creating a structural import gap and opportunity for domestic recyclers/processors. Copper prices reached $10,942/tonne on LME in late 2025, up ~26% year-on-year, driven by supply disruptions.

Zinc: India's zinc demand is projected to double over the next 5–10 years, driven by galvanising of infrastructure steel. Refined zinc production globally fell ~2% in 2025 despite mine output growth, as environmental regulations constrained capacity. Zinc prices climbed to ~$3,094/tonne in 2025.

Outside View Verdict: The industry's median ROCE of ~20–22% (top 10 companies) is above the Nalanda minimum threshold of 20%. However, this masks extreme dispersion — the bottom quartile earns well below the cost of capital. The sector as a whole is a mixed-quality industry where a handful of well-run recyclers and specialty wire manufacturers earn excellent returns, while commodity-processing lookalikes destroy value.

Key structural risks: 1. Chinese import pressure: Low-cost aluminium product imports from China undercut domestic pricing. The Aluminium Association of India (AAI) in October 2025 urged the government to raise import duties to 15% and tighten scrap norms. 2. Energy cost concentration: Aluminium smelting/recycling is energy-intensive. Companies without captive power or long-term PPAs face margin volatility. 3. Working capital trap: Raw material price spikes (LME) can rapidly expand working capital needs, generating cash flow losses even when accounting profits look healthy. 4. Fragmentation risk: Low barriers to entry at the commodity end of the spectrum allow perpetual margin compression as new participants enter during up-cycles.

Industry Rating: MODERATE QUALITY — passes the Nalanda outside-view test for selective stock picking but demands exceptional individual business quality to justify investment.

PART 4: KEY DARWIN PRINCIPLES APPLIED TO THIS SECTOR

The Bumblebee Test (Capital Preservation)

Of 25 companies, 17 have negative FCF — the single most important warning in a recycling/processing sector. The Darwin investor's instinct is to reject businesses that convert profits on paper into working capital holes. The few companies with sustained positive FCF (Parmeshwar, Century Extrusions, Baroda Extrusion, Sharvaya on a small base) stand out immediately.

The Siberian Fox Test (ROCE as the Primary Filter)

Selecting for ROCE >20% brought this universe from 25 companies to ~10. Selecting for ROCE >30% with positive FCF reduced it to 4–5. This is exactly how the Siberian fox experiment works — one filter eliminates most candidates while surfacing a small, high-quality subset.

The Cicada Test (Patience and Laziness)

The only conviction BUY here — Parmeshwar Metal — is a ₹335 Cr market cap copper wire rod company. Not exciting, not on CNBC, not in any thematic ETF. This is precisely where patient capital finds value. The Darwin investor buys it, files it away, and checks back in one annual report cycle.

Proximate vs. Ultimate Causes

The entire sector has benefited from proximate tailwinds (LME copper +26%, EV theme, PLI schemes). The Darwin investor ignores these and asks: which of these businesses will still be earning 30%+ ROCE when copper is at $7,000/tonne and the EV hype has normalised? The answer points to companies with structural scrap-sourcing advantages, certified quality, and proven capital discipline — not those riding commodity price cycles.
