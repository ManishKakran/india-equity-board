74. Heavy Electrical Equipment Companies — The Darwin Investor Analysis

Applying "What I Learned About Investing from Darwin" (Pulak Prasad / Nalanda Capital) framework to all 47 listed Indian Heavy Electrical Equipment companies

Analysis date: 23 June 2026 Current Sensex TTM PE (benchmark for Gate 2.4): ~20.1x (BSE Sensex, early-June 2026) Nalanda's target entry zone: 25–30% discount to this = ~14.0x–15.0x TTM PE Nalanda's stated investable universe filter: Market cap >$100M (~₹850 Cr at current USD/INR)

Framework discipline note: every threshold and verdict label below is taken word-for-word from the book (see source prompt). Where a company's facts create genuine tension with the literal text of the framework (e.g., MNC-subsidiary ownership, EPC-model "competitive advantage"), this is flagged explicitly rather than silently resolved — no interpretive smoothing.

PART A — INDUSTRY OVERVIEW (updated, June 2026)

A.1 What's happening in the sector right now

India's transmission & distribution (T&D) capex cycle, which began in FY22–23, remains the central demand driver. Motilal Oswal/Nuvama research confirms the National Electricity Plan envisages ~₹9 trillion (~$94bn) of transmission capex through 2032, and the cycle has already strengthened order books, revenues and margins for sector participants, although FY26 saw weaker ordering activity with only 16 schemes awarded versus 45 in FY25 — attributed to temporary execution and manufacturing capacity constraints rather than a structural demand problem.

Three structural tailwinds stand out: - Global transformer shortage: demand from the US and Europe is surging because of renewable energy integration, data centre expansion, industrial electrification, EV charging infrastructure and the replacement of ageing grids, pushing global buyers toward Indian manufacturers and supporting pricing power and export order books for the whole value chain. - Government capex push: in the Union Budget for FY2027, the government proposed capital expenditure of ₹12.2 lakh crore, 12% higher than earlier estimates, with high-voltage T&D companies reporting new orders up 33% YoY, sales up 39% YoY, and margins expanding to 20.3%. - Industry-wide growth runway: McKinsey estimates Indian electrical-equipment demand will grow 11–13% annually through 2035, tripling consumption to more than $170 billion, with transmission and distribution accounting for nearly 40% of the market.

The catch for a Darwin-style buyer: Nuvama/brokerage research explicitly flags that transformer players should deliver strong earnings growth between 2025 and 2028, although valuations are no longer cheap. This single sentence sums up the entire conclusion of this report — the operating story is excellent, but it has already been priced in, almost everywhere.

A.2 Outside View / Convergence Test (book's required first step before analysing any individual company)

"If the industry allows its companies to make money consistently, we love it; if not, we better have a perfect reason for spending even one minute analysing a business."

Splitting the 47-company universe into sub-industries and asking "does this industry structurally let participants earn >cost of capital?":

Grid/T&D MNC majors (Hitachi Energy, ABB, GE Vernova T&D, Siemens Energy, Schneider) ~30–76% (2) Industry uniformly attractive, but capital intensity and scale favour incumbents — explains the high ROCE and the market's willingness to pay up.

Conclusion: the transformer/grid-equipment/turbine core of this sector passes the outside-view test cleanly — this is consistent with the high median ROCE seen across most names with market cap above the investable threshold. The EPC/tower sub-segment is more mixed and requires picking the clear leader. The solar value-chain names embedded in this list are a different, faster-changing industry and are treated accordingly below.

PART C — GATE 1: AVOID BIG RISKS (the 21 survivors)

C.1 Clean disqualifications (fails a hard Gate 1 filter despite passing the quant screen)

BHEL — DISQUALIFY (Filter 1.1). Government-owned enterprise. Book is explicit: "We will not invest in a government-owned enterprise at any price." No further analysis needed (also fails ROCE at 8.51%).

CG Power & Industrial Solutions — DISQUALIFY (Filters 1.1 and 1.2). This is a textbook case the book itself describes. CG Power (under its prior promoter, the Avantha Group/Gautam Thapar) was the subject of a SEBI suo motu investigation into financial irregularities, including the inflation of assets and diversion of funds amounting to approximately ₹2,435 crore, resulting in a five-year ban on Thapar from securities markets and penalties totalling ₹30.15 crore on 11 entities. The Murugappa Group's subsequent turnaround — acquiring a 57% stake for ₹800 crore in November 2020 and turning the company net debt-free by FY22 — is a genuinely impressive piece of corporate restoration, and current governance is excellent. But the framework's Filter 1.2 exists precisely for this situation: "For every Tesla and Eicher, hundreds of unproven business models and turnaround stories are unceremoniously consigned to the dustbin of history." An investment thesis here is dependent on continued execution of a turnaround under new ownership, not multi-decade demonstrated excellence under stable promoters — a disqualifying pattern regardless of how well it has worked since 2020 or how high current ROCE (27.01%) now reads.

Suzlon Energy — DISQUALIFY (Filters 1.2 and 1.3). Suzlon has been through two separate debt-restructuring/near-bankruptcy events — it defaulted on its obligations in 2019 due to a change in the bidding regime, and prior to that there was a huge debt load from aggressive debt-funded acquisitions, with a restructuring also undertaken in 2012/2020. It only became net debt-free in FY24. This is precisely the "history of restructuring of debt, twice in the past, due to sizeable debt-funded acquisitions" pattern. However strong the current order book and balance sheet look today, the company's own rating agency commentary cites this restructuring history as a continuing constraint. This is the definitional turnaround story the book warns against — DISQUALIFY regardless of current momentum.

GE Power India — DISQUALIFY (Filter 1.2 / Gate 2.1 sustained-ROCE test). The headline ROCE of 79.96% is real but the 5-year average ROE is −7.71% — meaning this is a business that swung from loss-making to suddenly very profitable, not one with a sustained >20% multi-year track record. It is also a legacy thermal/boiler EPC business tied to BHEL/NTPC coal-plant cycles — a segment with structurally declining new-build volumes in India as the grid shifts toward renewables. Single-year ROCE spikes off a low/negative base are exactly what Analysis 2.1 warns against: "Is ROCE sustainably high — not a one-off... result?"

Indosolar, Jyoti Structures, Solex Energy — DISQUALIFY (Filter 1.2 and/or 1.5). Indosolar's 123.8% ROCE is a turnaround off a multi-year loss base in a fast-changing solar-cell technology niche. Jyoti Structures is a well-known case that went through insolvency-resolution proceedings (NCLT) — a Filter 1.3 distress pattern. Solex Energy sits in solar-module manufacturing, a fast-changing, China-exposed, margin-volatile technology segment that fails the "slow-changing industry" pre-condition independent of any single year's numbers.

PART D — GATE 2: HIGH QUALITY AT A FAIR PRICE (detailed, for the strongest candidates)

The book's primary lens after Gate 1 is valuation discipline — and this is where almost every name in this sector currently fails, regardless of how clean the underlying business is. Below is the full Gate 2 workup for the names that cleared Gate 1 with no severe flags, followed by a summary table for the rest.

D.1 Triveni Turbine — the strongest fit in the sector

  • 2.1 ROCE: 35.86% latest, 25.40% 5yr ROE average — comfortably clears >20%, consistent with its position as the #2 global manufacturer of sub-100MW industrial steam turbines.
  • 2.2 Robustness: HIGH. Promoter holding 55.8% (Sawhney family); FCF positive across all windows (₹999cr/5yr, ₹423cr/3yr, ₹144cr latest); no debt; demerged in 2010 specifically to separate this focused single-business entity from the diversified Triveni Engineering & Industries (sugar) group, so the entity itself is not diversified. Notably, Pulak Prasad himself sits on Triveni Turbine's board as a Non-Executive Director, and Nalanda India Fund holds ~4% of the company — this is one of Nalanda's actual real-world portfolio holdings, which is about as direct a validation of framework-fit as is possible.
  • 2.3 Management: EXCELLENT — multi-decade stable family leadership, conservative balance sheet, demerger executed specifically to sharpen focus rather than diversify.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~64.9x vs Sensex ~20.1x — a ~3.2x premium, nowhere near the 25–30% discount target.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: No proximate fear driving the price down — this is simply a structurally excellent business trading at a structurally rich multiple in a hot capex-cycle sector. NO GAP — EXPENSIVE.
  • Gate 2 verdict: Quality YES. Price NO.

D.2 Voltamp Transformers — cleanest balance sheet in the sector

  • 2.1 ROCE: 24.08% — clears the bar; CARE Ratings confirms a strong net worth base of ₹1,353 crore with no outstanding fund-based debt, gearing of just 0.07x.
  • 2.2 Robustness: HIGH. Professionally managed, debt-free, AA-Stable rated — the highest credit rating in the Indian transformer industry; second-generation Patel family promoters running a conservative, internal-accruals-funded expansion; FCF positive across all windows (₹580cr/5yr, ₹373cr/3yr, ₹159cr latest).
  • 2.3 Management: EXCELLENT — explicit, rated-agency-documented "conservative policy on debt-fuelled growth," capacity expansion (14,000→20,000 MVA) funded entirely through internal accruals.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE ~34.8x vs Sensex ~20.1x — a ~74% premium to the index.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: NO GAP — EXPENSIVE.
  • Gate 2 verdict: Quality YES (arguably the cleanest Gate-1 profile of any name studied). Price NO.

D.3 Transrail Lighting — the only name close to the valuation target

  • 2.1 ROCE: 29.18% latest; independent commentary confirms ROCE averaged 23.2% from FY23–FY25, peaking at 25.5% in FY24 — a real, multi-year (if still short) track record.
  • 2.2 Robustness: MODERATE-TO-HIGH. Promoter entity Ajanma Holdings (founder Digambar Bagde) holds ~71%; CRISIL recently upgraded the long-term rating to AA-(Stable); FCF positive across all windows (₹752cr/5yr, ₹690cr/3yr, ₹150cr latest) — one of the strongest FCF profiles in the entire list. EPC/tendering business models generally carry a thinner moat than pure product manufacturers, but Transrail's backward integration into towers, conductors and monopoles adds real cost and lead-time advantages over pure-EPC peers. D/E of 0.3–0.36 is right at/marginally above the book's 0.3 ceiling, though falling.
  • 2.3 Management: GOOD — Bagde has led the T&D EPC business independently since 1984, even while its former corporate parent Gammon India went through unrelated financial distress; professional MD brought in from KEC International in 2020 strengthened the bench.
  • 2.4 Valuation: TTM PE of 16.72x vs Sensex ~20.1x — a ~17% discount to the index. This is the only name in the entire 47-company list trading below the current Sensex multiple. It is not yet at the full 25–30% target discount (~14.0–15.0x), but it is the closest any quality name comes.
  • 2.5 Proximate vs ultimate: Some genuine proximate softness — sector-wide FY26 ordering slowed (16 vs 45 schemes YoY per Motilal Oswal), which has likely weighed on the stock without reflecting any deterioration in Transrail's own execution or balance sheet. PROXIMATE FEAR — potential buying signal if conviction on quality is high enough.
  • Gate 2 verdict: Quality YES, with the D/E and short-track-record flags noted. Price — closest to target of anything studied, but not yet there.

PART E — GATE 3: PERMANENT OWNERSHIP CONVICTION (for the names that could plausibly reach Gate 3)

Applying "if the stock market closed for ten years tomorrow, would you be comfortable holding this business?":

  • Triveni Turbine — Yes. Industrial steam turbines for captive power, cogeneration and waste-heat recovery are not going away in a 10-year window, and the company's competitive position (top-2 globally in its niche) has a logical reason to persist. LONG-TERM CONVICTION.
  • Voltamp Transformers — Yes. Transformers are infrastructure-grade, multi-decade assets; the company's conservative, debt-free operating model is itself a structural advantage that compounds over cycles. LONG-TERM CONVICTION.
  • Transrail Lighting — Provisionally yes, with the caveat that EPC-model competitive advantage is inherently less durable than a pure product moat, and the public track record (2 years) is too short to fully test the Grant–Kurtén principle of "exploiting short-term fluctuations" with confidence. LONG-TERM CONVICTION, lower confidence than the two above.

For every other name in this list, the analysis does not reach Gate 3 — either Gate 1 disqualified it, or Gate 2's valuation/robustness/track-record gaps mean the question of permanent ownership is premature.

PART G — ACTIONABLE OUTPUT: WHAT TO ADD TO WATCHLIST / BUY-ON-WEAKNESS LIST

No company in this 47-name universe currently qualifies for BUY NOW — every name that clears Gate 1 and Gate 2 quality is trading at a meaningful premium to the 14.0–15.0x entry zone the framework requires. This is itself a finding: the heavy-electrical-equipment sector's structural re-rating around the T&D capex super-cycle has pushed essentially every clean business in the space well outside Nalanda's stated valuation discipline. Per the book, this is a reason to wait, not a reason to abandon the names — Nalanda "never sells on valuation" and treats overvaluation as a reason to watch, not avoid permanently.

PART H — KEY TAKEAWAYS

  1. The industry passes the outside-view test. Transformer, grid-equipment and turbine manufacturing structurally allow most established players to earn well above their cost of capital right now — this is not a "hope" sector.
  2. But the whole sector is expensive. Every single quality name studied trades at a premium to the Sensex, most by 2–8x. This is consistent with brokerage commentary that the T&D capex story is now well-known and largely priced.
  3. Promoter-pledge and debt-ceiling checks did real work here. Two names with excellent headline ROCE (Indo Tech Transformers, Skipper) were moved to AVOID purely on Filter 1.1/1.3 grounds — exactly the kind of screen the book argues prevents "falling for great stories without financial proof."
  4. Recency of listing is a genuine constraint, not a technicality. Several of the most attractive-looking businesses by the numbers (Atlanta Electricals, Quality Power, Transrail Lighting, TRIL) have 1–2 years of public-market history. The book's ROCE test wants 5–10 years; that simply isn't available yet for newly-listed names, however good FY23–25 looks.
  5. One real outlier: Transrail Lighting is the only name in 47 trading below the current Sensex multiple, despite genuinely strong ROCE and FCF — worth tracking closely as the single most price-attractive quality candidate the sector currently offers.
